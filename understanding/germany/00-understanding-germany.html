Dynasties, Faith and Territory — Five Stages of History

How did a long-fragmented political space in Central Europe become modern Germany through dynastic rivalry, religious reform, war and unification? This account follows five historical stages, each beginning with an overview before exploring individual topics.

Antiquity–1992 | Five historical stages, with thematic appendices | European History · Germany

Cover image: the Reichstag building, Berlin, 2019 · Dietmar Rabich / Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Before you begin: distinguish three kinds of territory

Cultural and linguistic areas: places where German-speaking populations lived, which did not always belong to the same state.

Imperial political territory: the Holy Roman Empire included German lands, but also Bohemia and parts of Italy.

Dynastic possessions: one family could rule several countries. Habsburg rule in Spain did not make Spain German territory.

Marriage did not automatically merge states. A personal union meant that two political entities shared a monarch while retaining their own laws and institutions.

One timeline for the whole story

  • 843 Treaty of Verdun — East Francia emerges
  • 962 Otto I crowned emperor — a conventional starting point for the Holy Roman Empire
  • 1356 Golden Bull — seven prince-electors
  • 1517 Luther's Ninety-five Theses — the Reformation
  • 1648 Peace of Westphalia
  • 1806 Dissolution of the First Empire
  • 1871 Proclamation at Versailles — the Second Empire established
  • 1918 / 1919 Monarchy collapses and a republic is proclaimed / the Weimar Constitution is adopted
  • 1933 Hitler comes to power
  • 1945 Unconditional surrender
  • 1961 Berlin Wall built
  • 1990–1992 Reunification, followed by the entry into force of the final international settlement and border treaty

How to read this history

The five stages organize changes in political institutions; they are not five successive versions of an unchanging German state. Ancient Germanic peoples, the medieval empire and modern Germany belong to different historical categories. Neither 843 nor 962 was a modern national founding ceremony, and the outcome of 1871 was not predetermined.

Read the main chapters in order: Frankish inheritance → an elective and composite empire → competing projects for national unity → war, democracy and dictatorship → occupation, division and democratic reunification. Within the long imperial chapter, dynastic and institutional sections overlap in time; their date ranges make that overlap explicit. The separate dynastic appendix follows royal connections beyond 1806 without interrupting the main narrative.

For precise date relationships, use the chronology appendix. It distinguishes an election from a coronation, a proclamation from a constitution, and a treaty's signature from its entry into force. Modern place names orient the reader; they do not imply that modern national borders existed in earlier periods.
