# 01 Stage 1 Foundations and the Frankish Era

Germany and France both have roots in the Frankish kingdom. The ancient Germanic tribes had no unified state. After the Franks established their kingdom, the Treaty of Verdun divided the empire into three in 843. East Francia became an important foundation for the formation of Germany.

**AD 9** Battle of the Teutoburg Forest · **800** Charlemagne crowned emperor · **843** Treaty of Verdun

## 1.1 Germanic tribes and the Frankish kingdom

**Antiquity–800**

The ancient Germanic peoples comprised changing groups, not a unified nation or a direct equivalent of today's Germans. In AD 9, a coalition led by Arminius defeated three Roman legions under Varus in the **Battle of the Teutoburg Forest**. Rome continued campaigning afterward, so this was a major setback rather than an immediate and permanent end to every Roman presence east of the Rhine. It cannot be treated as Germany's founding.

The Franks subsequently established a kingdom in western Europe. Clovis of the Merovingian dynasty accepted Nicene Christianity, commonly described in this setting as Catholic Christianity, strengthening ties with bishops and the Romanized population. The precise date of his baptism is debated; the traditional date of 496 should not be presented as certain.

The Carolingians replaced the Merovingians as kings in 751. Charlemagne expanded the realm through conquest, including the Lombard kingdom in 774 and the long Saxon wars of 772–804. Forced conversion and violence accompanied that expansion. Pope Leo III crowned him emperor at Rome on 25 December 800. Aachen was a principal royal residence, not a modern national capital.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Territory · A comparison with the modern map**

Charlemagne's changing realm covered much of present-day France and Germany, parts of the Low Countries, the Alpine regions and northern and central Italy, among other areas. The conquest of Saxony extended Frankish power into northern Germany. These are approximate modern reference points, not the boundaries of modern states; Brittany, the Iberian frontier and other borderlands had differing relationships to Frankish authority.

## 1.2 843: the Treaty of Verdun divides the empire

**843**

| Political entity | Approximate geographical extent | Subsequent significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| West Francia | Centred on much of present-day France | An important foundation for the formation of France |
| East Francia | Mainly east of the Rhine, including Saxon, East Frankish, Bavarian and Alemannic regions; also Mainz, Worms and Speyer and associated lands west of the Rhine | An important foundation for the formation of Germany; the Rhine was not an exact national frontier |
| Middle Francia | From the North Sea coast through Lorraine and Burgundy to Italy | Subsequently fragmented; long contested by powers to its east and west |

The treaty followed civil war among the sons of Louis the Pious, who died in 840; it did not divide Charlemagne's possessions immediately after his death in 814. Lothair retained the imperial title without thereby exercising effective sovereignty over his brothers' kingdoms.

## 1.3 From East Francia to the Ottonians

843–962

Middle Francia fragmented after Lothair's death in 855. The settlements of Meerssen (870) and Ribemont (880) changed the division of Lotharingia, while Burgundy and Italy followed distinct paths. The map of 843 was therefore a starting configuration, not a permanent boundary settlement.

When Louis the Child, the last East Frankish Carolingian king, died in 911, leading nobles elected Conrad I. Henry I of the Saxon Liudolfing family succeeded in 919, initially relying especially on Saxon and Frankish support; recognition across the kingdom was a process. His son Otto I became king in 936 and emperor in 962. “East Frankish” and “German” describe a gradual historical transition, not a country renamed on a single day.

## Marriage, conversion and royal power

Clovis's wife Clotilde came from the Burgundian royal family and was Catholic. Their marriage illustrates the connection between dynastic alliances and religion, although Clovis's conversion also had political and social dimensions.

Charlemagne married a daughter of the Lombard king Desiderius, later repudiated the marriage, and conquered the Lombard kingdom in 774. Marriage could temporarily ease relations without eliminating struggles for power.
