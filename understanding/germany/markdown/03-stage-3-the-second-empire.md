# 03 Stage 3 The Second Empire: Birth of a Nation State

The German Confederation created after the Congress of Vienna left a fundamental question unanswered: which lands should a united Germany include? Through the customs union, three dynastic wars and Bismarck's political manoeuvring, Prussia pursued a “Lesser German” solution excluding Austria. The German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles in 1871 and rapidly became an industrial power.

**1815** German Confederation · **1834** Customs union · **1866** Austro-Prussian War · **1871** Empire proclaimed

## 3.1 Confederation, customs union and competing plans for unification

1815–1866

The **German Confederation** of 1815 comprised 39 members. It included much of present-day Germany, extensive areas of Austria and the Czech lands, Luxembourg and Liechtenstein, among other territories, but not all the lands of Austria or Prussia.

**Territory · Confederation borders and kingdom borders did not coincide**

Habsburg possessions such as Hungary lay outside the confederation, as did Prussian territories including East Prussia and Posen at its formation. This made the geographical scope of a united Germany a political problem.

The **German Customs Union (Zollverein)**, established in 1834 under Prussian leadership and without Austria, strengthened economic connections. In 1848, the Frankfurt National Assembly attempted constitutional unification, but its plan failed the following year when the Prussian king refused the imperial crown offered by the assembly.

| Plan for unification | Central proposal | Main difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greater Germany | Include Austria in the settlement | How to deal with the Habsburgs' multinational possessions |
| Lesser Germany | Exclude Austria and unite under Prussian leadership | What institutions should unite the remaining states |

#### Wilhelm I: Versailles hosted an imperial proclamation

Wilhelm I had already held his **coronation as king of Prussia** in Königsberg in 1861. The ceremony at Versailles on 18 January 1871 was the **proclamation of the German Emperor (Kaiserproklamation)**, not a medieval coronation at which a pope placed an imperial crown on his head.

The Franco-Prussian War was still underway, and Versailles housed German military headquarters. German princes and military representatives attended in the Hall of Mirrors. Bismarck read a proclamation to the German people, and the grand duke of Baden led the acclamation. The date echoed the anniversary of the Prussian royal coronation on 18 January 1701.

The new state's creation involved treaties and constitutional changes. The relevant constitutional arrangements had already taken effect on 1 January 1871; 18 January became the most prominent symbolic moment. The formal title was “German Emperor” (*Deutscher Kaiser*), a federal office held by the king of Prussia while the other states retained their own standing.

## 3.3 The Second Empire: federal monarchy and industrial power

1871–1918

**The Second Empire was the German Empire of 1871–1918.** The Prussian king also served as emperor, and Prussia possessed overwhelming advantages in territory, population and military strength. Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg and other states retained their royal houses and a measure of autonomy.

**Territory · Germany in 1871**

The empire included much of present-day Germany, together with East Prussia, most of West Prussia, Posen, Silesia, eastern Pomerania, North Schleswig and Alsace-Lorraine, among other territories.

It **excluded** Austria, Bohemia, Luxembourg and Liechtenstein. Unification incorporated many non-German speakers while leaving large German-speaking populations outside its borders.

The Reichstag was elected by universal adult male suffrage, but the chancellor was primarily responsible to the emperor. Industrialization rapidly advanced steel, chemicals, electrical engineering and scientific research, while social insurance developed gradually. Bismarck's **Kulturkampf**, or “culture struggle”, restricted Catholic Church influence but strengthened political mobilization by the Catholic Centre Party.

Overseas colonial expansion extended German power into Africa and the Pacific. Colonies must be distinguished from the empire's European territory. Naval expansion and *Weltpolitik*, its pursuit of a global role, also intensified competition with other great powers.
