# 03 Stage 3 The Second Empire: Birth of a Nation State

The German Confederation created after the Congress of Vienna left a fundamental question unanswered: which lands should a united Germany include? Through the customs union, the wars of unification and Bismarck's political manoeuvring, Prussia pursued a “Lesser German” solution excluding Austria. The German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles in 1871 and rapidly became an industrial power.

**1815** German Confederation · **1834** Customs union · **1866** Austro-Prussian War · **1871** Empire proclaimed

## From 1806 to 1815: reform, resistance and reconstruction

The dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire did not immediately produce the German Confederation. Prussia's defeats at Jena and Auerstedt in October 1806 exposed weaknesses in its army and administration. Reforms associated with Stein, Hardenberg and military reformers followed, while Napoleonic rule elsewhere combined legal and administrative changes with military demands and foreign domination.

Napoleon's defeat in Russia in 1812 and the coalition victory at Leipzig in October 1813 undermined French predominance. The Congress of Vienna of 1814–1815 reorganized Europe; the German Federal Act of 8 June 1815 established a confederation of sovereign states, not a restored Holy Roman Empire or a unified nation state. Prussia gained extensive Rhineland and Westphalian territories and part of Saxony, creating important foundations for its later industrial strength.

## 3.1 Confederation, customs union and competing plans for unification

1815–1866

The **German Confederation**, established in 1815, is conventionally summarized as a union of 39 states; membership changed, and counts depend on the date and treatment of jointly ruled territories. Its Federal Assembly met at Frankfurt under Austrian presidency.  It included much of present-day Germany, extensive areas of Austria and the Czech lands, Luxembourg and Liechtenstein, among other territories, but not all the lands of Austria or Prussia.

**Territory · Confederation borders and kingdom borders did not coincide**

Habsburg possessions such as Hungary lay outside the confederation, as did Prussian territories including East Prussia and Posen at its formation. This made the geographical scope of a united Germany a political problem.

The **German Customs Union (Zollverein)** took effect on 1 January 1834 under Prussian leadership, without Austria. It reduced internal customs barriers among participating states, but economic integration did not automatically settle political borders or ensure Prussian-led unity.

The revolutions of 1848 linked demands for constitutional government, civil rights and national unity. The Frankfurt National Assembly first met in St Paul's Church on 18 May 1848, adopted a constitution in March 1849 and offered the imperial crown to Frederick William IV of Prussia. His rejection was a decisive setback, alongside divisions within the movement and the recovery of monarchical military power. The revolutions failed to establish the proposed national order, but their constitutional and rights debates outlasted that defeat.

## 3.2 Bismarck's path to unity and the imperial proclamation

1864–1871

### 1866

Prussia defeated Austria, decisively at Königgrätz (Sadowa) on 3 July. The German Confederation dissolved. Prussia annexed Hanover, Electoral Hesse, Nassau and Frankfurt, while Austria was excluded from the subsequent Prussian-led union. This political outcome should not be described as Austria ceasing to have German-speaking inhabitants.

The constitution of the North German Confederation took effect on 1 July 1867. Despite the conventional English name “Confederation”, it was a federal state, unlike the looser association of 1815.

### 1870–1871

France declared war on Prussia on 19 July 1870 after the Spanish succession crisis and Bismarck's handling of the Ems Dispatch. The war encouraged the southern German states to join. On 18 January 1871, Wilhelm I was proclaimed German Emperor at Versailles.

### Wilhelm I: Versailles hosted an imperial proclamation

Wilhelm I had already held his **coronation as king of Prussia** in Königsberg in 1861. The ceremony at Versailles on 18 January 1871 was the **proclamation of the German Emperor (Kaiserproklamation)**, not a medieval coronation at which a pope placed an imperial crown on his head.

The Franco-Prussian War was still underway, and Versailles housed German military headquarters. German princes and military representatives attended in the Hall of Mirrors. Bismarck read a proclamation to the German people, and the grand duke of Baden led the acclamation. The date echoed the anniversary of the Prussian royal coronation on 18 January 1701.

The new state's creation involved treaties and constitutional changes. The relevant constitutional arrangements had already taken effect on 1 January 1871; 18 January became the most prominent symbolic moment. The revised imperial constitution was dated 16 April and entered into force on 4 May 1871. The formal title was “German Emperor” (*Deutscher Kaiser*), a federal office held by the king of Prussia while the other states retained their own standing.

## 3.3 The Second Empire: federal monarchy and industrial power

1871–1918

**The Second Empire was the German Empire of 1871–1918.** The Prussian king also served as emperor, and Prussia possessed overwhelming advantages in territory, population and military strength. Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg and other states retained their royal houses and a measure of autonomy.

**Territory · Germany in 1871**

The empire included much of present-day Germany, together with East Prussia, most of West Prussia, Posen, Silesia, eastern Pomerania, North Schleswig and Alsace-Lorraine, among other territories.

It **excluded** Austria, Bohemia, Luxembourg and Liechtenstein. Unification incorporated many non-German speakers while leaving large German-speaking populations outside its borders.

The Reichstag was elected through a broadly equal, direct male franchise from age 25, subject to legal exclusions. Women could not vote, and men serving in the armed forces could not exercise the vote while on active service. This differed from Prussia's unequal three-class electoral system for its state parliament. The chancellor depended on the emperor's confidence rather than a parliamentary majority until the constitutional reforms of October 1918; legislation nevertheless required parliamentary participation. Industrialization rapidly advanced steel, chemicals, electrical engineering and scientific research, while social insurance developed gradually. Bismarck's **Kulturkampf**, or “culture struggle”, restricted Catholic Church influence but strengthened political mobilization by the Catholic Centre Party.

Overseas colonial expansion extended German power into Africa and the Pacific. Colonies must be distinguished from the empire's European territory. Naval expansion and *Weltpolitik*, its pursuit of a global role, also intensified competition with other great powers.


**Modernization and exclusion coexisted.** The Anti-Socialist Law of 1878–1890 restricted socialist organizations and publications, while social insurance laws introduced sickness coverage in 1883, accident coverage in 1884, and old-age and disability insurance in 1889. Nation-building also involved pressure on Polish-speaking populations and other minorities. Industrial development was not equivalent to full political equality.

Colonial rule began in the 1880s and involved land dispossession, forced labour and violence. In German South West Africa, now Namibia, German forces committed genocide against the Herero and Nama in 1904–1908. This belongs to the empire's history alongside economic growth and social insurance.
