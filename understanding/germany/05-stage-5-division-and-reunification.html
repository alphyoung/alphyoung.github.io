05 Stage 5 Division, Reconstruction and Reunification

After 1945, occupation, changes to the eastern frontier and population displacement transformed Germany. The Cold War helped produce two states in 1949. Reunification on 3 October 1990 followed peaceful protest, democratic elections and international negotiation; the associated treaties completed their entry-into-force procedures in 1991 and 1992.

1945 Occupation · 1949 Two states · 1961 Berlin Wall · 1989 Wall opened · 1990 Reunification · 1991–1992 International treaties enter into force

5.1 Defeat, occupation and the eastern frontier

1945–1949

The Allies assumed supreme authority in Germany in June 1945. At Potsdam in July–August, the United States, United Kingdom and Soviet Union agreed on principles for occupation policy. Germany was administered in four zones, including a French zone; Berlin, inside the Soviet zone geographically, was separately divided into four sectors. Occupation boundaries were not yet the boundaries of two German states.

Most former German territories east of the Oder and western Neisse rivers were placed under Polish administration; northern East Prussia, including Königsberg (renamed Kaliningrad in 1946), came under Soviet control. Potsdam left final territorial settlement to a later peace settlement. Poland's westward shift was also linked to its loss of eastern territories to the Soviet Union. Austria re-established its republic in April 1945 but remained under four-power occupation until the State Treaty arrangements of 1955 restored full sovereignty.

Millions of Germans fled or were expelled during the war's final stages and afterward. Other populations, including Poles displaced from territories annexed by the Soviet Union, were also uprooted. These movements transformed Central Europe's population. They should be explained in the context of Nazi aggression and occupation, without treating violence against civilians as justified or reducing different experiences to a single undifferentiated story.

Accountability accompanied political reconstruction. The International Military Tribunal trial of major war criminals at Nuremberg began on 20 November 1945 and concluded its verdicts on 1 October 1946; other trials followed. Criminal trials, denazification and wider social reckoning were distinct processes, and occupation-zone practices differed.

Source: War crimes trials.

5.2 Why two German states emerged

1945–1949

Occupation policies diverged. In the western zones, parliamentary institutions and economic reconstruction developed within an increasingly western alignment. In the Soviet zone, land reform, nationalization and the forced merger of the Communist and Social Democratic parties into the Socialist Unity Party (SED) in 1946 helped consolidate a different system. Neither outcome can be explained by a timeless division between two kinds of Germans.

The Marshall Plan, western currency reform in June 1948 and the dispute over Berlin intensified the break between the Allies. The Soviet blockade of western access to West Berlin lasted from 24 June 1948 to 12 May 1949; the western airlift sustained the city. The blockade was not the Berlin Wall, which came thirteen years later.

The Basic Law was promulgated on 23 May 1949, conventionally the founding date of the Federal Republic of Germany, with Bonn as the provisional seat of government. The German Democratic Republic was established on 7 October 1949, with its government in East Berlin. Both remained subject to important Allied powers and constraints; neither date meant immediate, unrestricted sovereignty.

Sources: Berlin blockade · Marshall Plan and currency reform · Chronology of 1949.

5.3 Two political systems within the Cold War

1949–1989

Dimension Federal Republic (West Germany) German Democratic Republic (East Germany)
Political institutions Federal parliamentary democracy under the Basic Law; Adenauer was the first federal chancellor SED dictatorship behind formally multiparty institutions; opposition, elections and public life were controlled
Economy Social market economy, western investment and trade, rapid but uneven reconstruction Planned economy, nationalization and collectivization, with both social provision and structural shortages
International alignment NATO membership in 1955; a founding member of the European Economic Community in 1957 Member of the Warsaw Pact from 1955 and integrated into the Soviet bloc
Society and religion Christian Democratic politics bridged some Catholic-Protestant divisions; migration and social change widened political participation Increasing secularization; some Protestant churches provided limited space for peace, human-rights and opposition groups
Confronting the Nazi past Trials, reparations, research and education developed amid resistance and continuities in personnel Official antifascism coexisted with selective remembrance and political repression; it did not amount to a complete reckoning with society's past

On 17 June 1953, protests in East Germany developed into a broad uprising and were suppressed by Soviet forces and East German security organs. On 13 August 1961, the GDR began closing the border around West Berlin and building the Wall to stop mass emigration. The Wall divided Berlin and encircled its western sectors; the wider inter-German border was a separate, increasingly fortified barrier. West Berlin remained under a special Allied status, not that of an ordinary federal state.

The Saar joined the Federal Republic politically on 1 January 1957; its economic and currency integration followed on 6 July 1959. West Germany gained extensive sovereignty in 1955, but Allied reservations concerning Berlin and Germany as a whole remained. These qualifications matter when comparing 1949, 1955 and 1990–1991.

Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik sought détente and practical relations. The Moscow and Warsaw treaties were signed in 1970; the Basic Treaty between the two German states followed in 1972 and entered into force in 1973. Both states joined the United Nations in 1973. The GDR had recognized the Oder-Neisse border in the Görlitz Agreement of 1950; West Germany recognized its inviolability in the Warsaw Treaty of 1970, while the final all-German settlement was completed in 1990–1992.

Sources: The German question · 1955 sovereignty and its limits · Chronology of 1959.

5.4 Peaceful revolution, accession and the final settlement

1989–1992

Mass emigration, demonstrations, economic problems and changes in Soviet policy undermined the SED regime. The peaceful mass demonstration in Leipzig on 9 October 1989 was a crucial turning point. On 9 November, confusion surrounding Günter Schabowski's announcement of travel rules and pressure from crowds led to the opening of border crossings. The Wall's opening did not itself create a single state, and protesters did not all initially seek the same political outcome.

Date Event What it did—and did not—mean

31 August 1990 Unification Treaty signed Regulated domestic legal and institutional arrangements; distinct from the international Two Plus Four Treaty
12 September 1990 Two Plus Four Treaty signed in Moscow The two German states and four powers agreed the external settlement, borders and security conditions
3 October 1990 GDR accession and German reunification The GDR ceased to exist; five re-established eastern Länder became federal states and Berlin was united
14 November 1990 German-Polish Border Treaty signed in Warsaw Confirmed the existing border; this was not the earlier 1970 Warsaw Treaty
15 March 1991 Two Plus Four Treaty entered into force Formally ended the four powers' rights and responsibilities, which had been suspended for unification
16 January 1992 German-Polish Border Treaty entered into force Completed this treaty's ratification and entry-into-force process

The legal mechanism was the GDR's accession to the Federal Republic, not five already sovereign countries independently joining a new federation. The five Länder were re-established in connection with accession, after having lost their effective administrative role in the GDR's 1952 centralization. Berlin's two parts together formed a federal state. Reunification did not restore the borders of 1871 or 1937.

Sources: Volkskammer accession decision · Unification Treaty · Two Plus Four signature · Treaty entry into force · German-Polish border chronology.

Political unity did not eliminate economic or social differences. Reunification brought democratic rights and new opportunities alongside deindustrialization, unemployment and difficult institutional transitions in eastern Germany. The story therefore ends with an achieved constitutional and international settlement, not a claim that all consequences of division had disappeared.
