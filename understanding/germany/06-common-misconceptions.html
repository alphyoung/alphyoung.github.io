06 Common Misconceptions

Many common mistakes in reading German history arise from confusing dynasties, empires and nation states. Each point below is explored in the relevant chapter; this section brings them together for review.

Was the Holy Roman Empire simply Germany's old name?

Not exactly. The empire centred on German lands but also included the Kingdom of Bohemia, with its large non-German-speaking population, as well as northern Italian, Burgundian and other territories. The full name “Holy Roman Empire” took shape much later and was not in use in 962. It was a political framework encompassing several kingdoms, not a single nation state.

Did Habsburg rule make Spain German territory?

No. Dynastic possessions and “Germany” were different things: one family could rule several states. Charles V inherited several territories at different dates, and Ferdinand took over the Austrian hereditary lands in 1521–1522. These lands did not merge into one unified state. Spain and its American colonies did not become German territory, nor did Habsburg rule bring Hungary into the empire.

Did a royal marriage merge two countries?

No. Marriage could provide an inheritance claim, but succession rules, noble recognition, negotiation and even war determined whether it could be realized. In a personal union, such as that between Britain and Hanover, two entities shared a monarch while retaining separate governments and finances. When Victoria succeeded in Britain in 1837, different succession rules brought a different ruler to Hanover.

Was Austria always one of the empire's seven electorates?

No. Austria was absent from the seven seats defined in the Golden Bull of 1356: three archbishops, the king of Bohemia, the count palatine of the Rhine, the duke of Saxony and the margrave of Brandenburg. The Habsburgs later obtained the Bohemian crown and its associated vote.

Were Bavaria and the Palatinate the same thing?

No. Both belonged to the Wittelsbach family, but the electoral vote of 1356 belonged to the Palatine branch. Electoral status was transferred to Bavaria in 1623, and a new seat was created for the Palatinate in 1648. “Saxony” also requires care: the early medieval duchy lay in the north, whereas the later electorate's centre was farther east. Neither is identical to the modern state of Saxony.

Did the religious settlement of 1555 establish religious freedom?

No. The Peace of Augsburg established an arrangement in which territorial rulers chose the confession for their lands. It did not provide modern individual religious freedom, and it did not yet include the Reformed confession. Confessional boundaries were also geographically interwoven, rather than following a simple north-south divide.

Did every area occupied by the Nazis become German territory?

Formal annexation, protectorates and military occupation must be distinguished. Austria and the Sudetenland were annexed in 1938, but the occupation of France, Norway or parts of the Soviet Union did not make those areas part of Germany's national territory. Occupation extended much farther than annexation.

Was Wilhelm I “crowned emperor” at Versailles on 18 January 1871?

The event was an imperial proclamation (Kaiserproklamation), not a medieval coronation. There was no papal crowning; the setting was German military headquarters during the Franco-Prussian War. The new state's constitutional arrangements had already taken effect on 1 January 1871. The ceremony of 18 January provided a symbolic moment, deliberately echoing the Prussian royal coronation of 1701.

Were “First”, “Second” and “Third Empire” official state names?

“First Empire” (962–1806) is a retrospective label. “Second Empire” refers to the German Empire of 1871–1918. “Third Reich” was a Nazi propaganda term: the regime restored neither an emperor nor dynastic rule. These labels do not establish an uninterrupted succession of three equivalent imperial systems. However, the transition from imperial monarchy to Weimar democracy and then Nazi dictatorship involved changes of regime within the German Reich, not three wholly unrelated states. “No institutional continuity at all” would also be misleading.

Did reunification restore the old empire's territory?

No. The GDR acceded to the Federal Republic on 3 October 1990; five eastern Länder were re-established and Berlin was united. The Two Plus Four Treaty was signed in September 1990 and took effect in March 1991; the German-Polish Border Treaty was signed in November 1990 and took effect in January 1992. The existing Oder-Neisse boundary was confirmed, without restoring the borders of 1871 or 1937.

Did the Peace of Westphalia create modern nation-state sovereignty in one step?

No. It preserved the empire, confirmed and qualified territorial rights, and regulated religious coexistence. Its several treaties must be distinguished. The imperial princes did not simply become completely unrestricted sovereign nation states.

Did the fall of the Berlin Wall mean reunification?

No. Border crossings opened on 9 November 1989. Elections, negotiations and separate domestic and international treaties followed before accession on 3 October 1990. Some treaty provisions completed their legal entry into force only in 1991–1992.

Is every traditional date equally certain?

No. Clovis's baptism is disputed; the start of the Great Interregnum varies by convention; 962 is a conventional period boundary. These differ from clearly documented signatures or votes. A date range or an explicit qualification is more accurate than invented precision.
