04 Stage 4 War, Collapse and Nazi Rule

Defeat in the First World War ended imperial monarchy and dynastic politics. The Treaty of Versailles redrew borders, while Weimar democracy struggled through crises before the Nazi regime destroyed it. The “Third Reich” of 1933–1945 brought annexation, occupation and genocide. Germany surrendered unconditionally in 1945.

1919 Treaty of Versailles · Weimar Constitution

4.1 The First World War and the collapse of monarchy

1914–1918

The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 triggered a diplomatic crisis, not an automatic chain reaction. Germany's “blank cheque” support encouraged Austria-Hungary, which declared war on Serbia on 28 July. Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August and France on 3 August, then invaded neutral Belgium on 4 August; Britain entered the war that day. Decisions by governments, military planning, alliance politics and imperial rivalry escalated the conflict. Royal kinship did not determine those decisions.

War on multiple fronts, the blockade, shortages and mass casualties strained German society. The military high command acquired exceptional influence, particularly under Hindenburg and Ludendorff from 1916. Defeat became clear in 1918 after the failure of the spring offensives and the Allied counteroffensive. The claim that an undefeated army was betrayed by civilians—the “stab-in-the-back” myth—misrepresented military defeat and was used against the republic.

The Kiel sailors' uprising in early November and the November Revolution spread across Germany. On 9 November 1918, Chancellor Max von Baden announced Wilhelm II's abdication without the emperor's authorization; Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed a republic in Berlin, while Karl Liebknecht proclaimed a socialist republic later that day. Wilhelm signed his formal abdication on 28 November. The military armistice had taken effect on 11 November; it was distinct from the later peace treaty. The states' monarchies also fell, ending royal marriage's constitutional role in German state succession.

Sources: Chronology of 1918 · Wilhelm II's abdication.

4.2 The Weimar Republic: democracy under pressure

1918–1933

The National Assembly elected on 19 January 1919 included women as voters and representatives for the first time in a German national election. It met at Weimar, adopted the constitution on 31 July, and President Friedrich Ebert signed it on 11 August; it took effect on 14 August. “Weimar Republic” names this political period, while the state's official name remained Deutsches Reich.

The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 and entered into force on 10 January 1920. Border changes and plebiscites were implemented at different dates, not all on the day of signature.

Germany lost approximately 13 per cent of its prewar European territory. Territorial losses and reparations became major themes of nationalist mobilization, but the republic's failure also involved domestic anti-democratic forces and economic crises.

Source: Federal Agency for Civic Education: Versailles and Weimar

Political violence and the hyperinflation of 1923 were followed by a period of relative stabilization in 1924–1929. Culture and science flourished, and parliamentary politics remained meaningful. The republic's collapse was not inevitable from its founding. Dependence on foreign credit, the Great Depression after 1929, unemployment, anti-democratic mobilization and government by presidential emergency decree after 1930 progressively undermined it. The Nazis exploited nationalism and antisemitism, while conservative elites helped bring Hitler into government in the mistaken belief that they could control him.

The Nazis were the largest party in the July and November 1932 Reichstag elections but won no absolute majority in either. Hitler was appointed chancellor by President Paul von Hindenburg on 30 January 1933; he was not elected chancellor by a majority of the population.

Source: How Hitler came to power.

4.3 Building dictatorship and pursuing expansion

1933–1939

“Third Reich” was a Nazi propaganda term. The regime did not restore an emperor or dynastic government.

After the Reichstag fire on 27 February, the decree of 28 February 1933 suspended fundamental rights. The Enabling Act was passed on 23 March under intimidation and exclusion of Communist deputies and promulgated on 24 March. It permitted government legislation without normal parliamentary consent. Arrests, the destruction of independent trade unions, the elimination of competing parties and the subordination of the states consolidated dictatorship. This was a process of coercion and institutional dismantling, not an ordinary democratic transfer of power.

Sources: 1933: key dates · The Enabling Act.

The Nazis courted, controlled and persecuted churches. Some individuals accommodated the regime, while others resisted or protected those being persecuted. The churches as a whole failed to stop persecution effectively. Nazi antisemitism centred on racial ideology while also exploiting longstanding European anti-Jewish prejudice.

The Nuremberg Laws of 15 September 1935 excluded Jews from full citizenship and prohibited marriages and sexual relations defined by the regime as crossing its racial categories. The pogrom of 9–10 November 1938, often called “Kristallnacht”, marked further escalation.

4.4 War of aggression, occupation and genocide

1939–1945

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939; Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September. The Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland on 17 September, following the German-Soviet pact and its secret division of spheres of influence. Germany's invasion began the Second World War in Europe; wars in Asia had begun earlier.

Germany conquered or occupied much of northern and western Europe in 1940. On 22 June 1941, it invaded the Soviet Union in a war of conquest and racial annihilation. Defeat at Stalingrad in 1943, Allied advances in the east and west, and the Normandy landings in June 1944 contributed to the regime's military collapse.

Military occupation extended much farther than formal annexation. Occupying France, Norway or parts of the Soviet Union did not turn all those areas into Germany's national territory.

Ghettos, deportations, forced labour, mass shootings and killing centres formed different but connected instruments of persecution and murder.

At the Wannsee Conference in Berlin on 20 January 1942, Reinhard Heydrich coordinated cooperation by state and party agencies in the murder of Europe's Jews. Mass killing was already underway: the conference did not initiate the Holocaust, and Hitler did not attend. The SS, police, civilian authorities, parts of the armed forces and collaborators contributed in differing roles.

Sources: World War II chronology · Wannsee conference documents.

The Nazis and their collaborators murdered approximately six million Jews and persecuted and killed Roma, Sinti, disabled people, Polish civilians, Soviet prisoners of war and other groups on a massive scale. Germany's unconditional surrender in 1945 ended Nazi rule and dismantled its order of annexation.

The German armed forces signed an unconditional surrender at Reims on 7 May 1945, effective at 23:01 Central European Time on 8 May. A further signing took place at Berlin-Karlshorst on the night of 8–9 May. The time difference helps explain the commemoration of victory on 8 May in much of Europe and 9 May in the Soviet tradition. These were military surrender acts, not a peace treaty restoring German sovereignty. The Allies assumed supreme authority on 5 June 1945.

The war's defeat brought liberation for those persecuted by Nazism and the end of the regime, alongside destruction, displacement and occupation. These experiences must not obscure responsibility for German aggression and Nazi crimes.

Additional sources: Adoption, signature and entry into force of the Weimar Constitution · German surrender in May 1945.
