04 Stage 4 War, Collapse and Nazi Rule

Defeat in the First World War ended imperial monarchy and dynastic politics. The Treaty of Versailles redrew borders, while Weimar democracy struggled through crises before the Nazi regime destroyed it. The “Third Reich” of 1933–1945 brought annexation, occupation and genocide. Germany surrendered unconditionally in 1945.

4.1 The First World War, the end of monarchy and the Weimar Republic

1914–1933

The First World War broke out in 1914. Germany's support for Austria-Hungary and its own strategic choices were important factors in the crisis's escalation. Kinship among Europe's royal families failed to prevent war.

The Kiel sailors' mutiny and the November Revolution of 1918 drove the collapse of monarchy. Wilhelm II abdicated, and the monarchies of the individual states fell in turn. Royal marriages thereafter ceased to have an institutional role in determining Germany's national succession.

Germany lost approximately 13 per cent of its prewar European territory. Territorial losses and reparations became major themes of nationalist mobilization, but the republic's failure also involved domestic anti-democratic forces and economic crises.

Source: Federal Agency for Civic Education: Versailles and Weimar

The Weimar Constitution of 1919 established democratic institutions and universal suffrage including women. Culture and science flourished for a time, but political violence, hyperinflation in 1923 and the Great Depression after 1929 repeatedly undermined democracy. The Nazis exploited unemployment, nationalism and antisemitism to expand their influence, while conservative elites mistakenly believed they could control Hitler.

4.2 Nazi rule: annexation, occupation and destruction

1933–1945

“Third Reich” was a Nazi propaganda term. The regime did not restore an emperor or dynastic government.

Hitler became chancellor in 1933, then used emergency measures and the Enabling Act to destroy democracy, repress opponents and weaken the autonomy of the federal states.

The Nazis courted, controlled and persecuted churches. Some individuals accommodated the regime, while others resisted or protected those being persecuted. The churches as a whole failed to stop persecution effectively. Nazi antisemitism centred on racial ideology while also exploiting longstanding European anti-Jewish prejudice.

Military occupation extended much farther than formal annexation. Occupying France, Norway or parts of the Soviet Union did not turn all those areas into Germany's national territory.

The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped Jews of rights, and the November pogrom of 1938, known as “Kristallnacht”, marked a further escalation of anti-Jewish violence. War and mass murder expanded after the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941. The Wannsee Conference of 1942 coordinated the policy of exterminating Europe's Jews that was already underway.

The Nazis and their collaborators murdered approximately six million Jews and persecuted and killed Roma, Sinti, disabled people, Polish civilians, Soviet prisoners of war and other groups on a massive scale. Germany's unconditional surrender in 1945 ended Nazi rule and dismantled its order of annexation.
