03 Stage 3 Re-drawing the Boundaries

1453 — 1648

Two things happened at once in this stage: the shared faith of Latin Christendom broke apart, and the Mediterranean-centred economy was displaced by the Atlantic sea routes. Meanwhile in the southeast, an Islamic empire ruled from Istanbul took the Balkans, most of Hungary and the northern Black Sea into its territory. Europe's outer boundaries and its internal map were redrawn within these two centuries.

  • 1492 Columbus reaches the Americas

3.1 Renaissance and humanism

14th — 16th century

The change had already been brewing in Italy. Wealthy Italian city-states — including the republics of Florence and Venice and the predominantly lordly and ducal regime of Milan — patronized artists and scholars, the Medici being the most famous of the patrons. Scholars collected and collated Greek and Roman texts afresh, producing humanism: a shift of attention from theological speculation toward human dignity, cultivation and public life.

Technology amplified the movement. Around 1450 Gutenberg improved movable-type printing, and books could be reproduced in quantity. Ideas no longer depended on manuscript and word of mouth; the speed and reach of their transmission were utterly transformed — which also made the rapid spread of the coming Reformation possible.

Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael, Machiavelli, Erasmus — they inherited antiquity while looking at reality with new eyes. The Renaissance did not cut itself off from the Middle Ages; it reorganized medieval nourishment in a new way.

The background to a rescue operation. Humanists were hunting down Greek manuscripts at exactly the moment when large numbers of Byzantine scholars were moving west with their books. This was no coincidence: after the fall of Constantinople in 1453 many Greek scholars took their manuscripts to Italy to make a living, carrying the original texts of Plato and Aristotle directly into the Latin world.

3.2 Oceanic voyages and colonial empires

1415 — 1600

The Ottomans took Constantinople in 1453 and thereafter controlled the trade routes between the eastern Mediterranean and Eurasia for a long time. Western merchants therefore turned increasingly to the sea — though to attribute overseas expansion entirely to "the blocked trade route" is a simplification: Portuguese voyages had begun as early as 1415. Portugal pushed south along the African coast, rounded the Cape of Good Hope and entered the Indian Ocean; Spain ventured westward — and in 1492 Columbus reached the Americas.

Over the following century Spain and Portugal were first to build transoceanic colonial empires, with the Dutch, English and French joining later. American silver poured into Europe and overturned prices and trade; between Africa and the Americas the transatlantic slave trade was forcibly assembled, with an estimated 12.5 million people forced aboard ships in Africa and about 10.7 million surviving to disembark in the Americas over the entire trade, roughly the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries; many also died during capture.

The deeper consequence of the voyages was a shift in economic gravity: the Mediterranean ceased to be Europe's centre, and the Atlantic ports — Lisbon, Seville, Amsterdam, London — rose in turn. Europe's internal balance of power began to tilt northwest.

On the phrase "the Age of Discovery": what was discovery for Europeans was the beginning of catastrophe for the indigenous peoples of the Americas — conquest, enslavement and disease cut their populations drastically. More and more textbooks now speak of "the age of oceanic exploration" or "colonial expansion", to hold within the frame the side that was written out.

3.3 Two meanings of 1453

1453 — 1571

For Latin Western Europe, 1453 is often only a period marker: the Middle Ages end here, the modern age begins here. But the same event has another face.

The fall of Constantinople ended a Byzantine Empire that had lasted nearly a thousand years. The last emperor, Constantine XI, died in the city, and the highest political protector of Orthodoxy vanished with him. Moscow gradually took up the idea of a "Third Rome", while the churches of Greece, Serbia and Bulgaria had to renegotiate their place under a Muslim sovereign.

At the same time, the Ottoman Empire became a European empire straddling three continents. Its capital, Istanbul (the former Constantinople), was one of Europe's largest cities; it ruled the Balkans and Anatolia directly, and held Wallachia, Moldavia and the Crimean Khanate as vassals; a single provincial governor could be richer than many a Western king. The Battle of Mohács in 1526 shattered the Hungarian army, after which Hungary was divided in three: the centre directly under Ottoman rule, the northwest to the Habsburgs, and the east forming the principality of Transylvania. In 1529 Ottoman forces besieged Vienna for the first time — the heart of central Europe facing troops from the southeast for the first time.

Only at the Battle of Lepanto in 1571 did a Christian allied fleet check Ottoman sea power in the Mediterranean (though its land superiority was untouched). For the next century and more, no political agenda in central or southeastern Europe could be drawn up without reference to this neighbour.

Relation to the Ottomans Region Rough form
Direct rule Most of the Balkans, Anatolia, central Hungary Governed as provinces, registered for taxation and military service
Vassalage Wallachia, Moldavia, the Crimean Khanate Tribute and troops, with considerable internal autonomy
Standing confrontation The Habsburgs, Venice, Poland–Lithuania War alternating with truce, borders shifting repeatedly
Watching from across the water England, France, the Nordic lands Trade and even alliance, sometimes jointly against the Habsburgs

A frequently overlooked fact: France allied with the Ottomans. In the sixteenth century, to break out of Habsburg encirclement, Francis I of France reached a cooperative arrangement with Suleiman I. This shows that "Christendom versus Islam" was never a solid bloc — state interest repeatedly outweighed religious alignment.

3.4 The Reformation: the fracture of a single faith

1517 — 1555

In 1517 Martin Luther issued the Ninety-five Theses, primarily challenging indulgences and related practices; justification by faith alone became central to his subsequent reforming teaching. The Bible was translated into German, and ordinary people could read it for themselves. Calvin built another reforming tradition in Geneva, while Henry VIII of England broke with Roman jurisdiction amid disputes over his annulment and royal authority; royal supremacy over the existing English Church was established in 1534. The religious unity Western Europe had kept for a thousand years was broken.

The Peace of Augsburg of 1555 established cuius regio, eius religio — whose realm, his religion. But this brought no freedom of individual conscience and left Calvinism out; grievances kept accumulating, and half a century later they erupted in a far larger war.

Eastern Europe at the same time was another map entirely. While the West went to war over faith, Poland–Lithuania (formally united in 1569) accommodated Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and Europe's largest Jewish community, and adopted religious toleration in 1573; the Hussites of Bohemia had already gone their own way; parts of Hungary turned Protestant; while Russia and the Orthodox Balkan peoples carried on, respectively through the Moscow patriarchate and the Ottoman millet system. The Reformation was not a story about all of Europe — it had its own terrain.

3.5 The Thirty Years' War and the Westphalian system

1618 — 1648

The Thirty Years' War of 1618–1648 was the general eruption of religious and dynastic conflict: beginning with a rebellion in Bohemia, it drew in Spain, Denmark, Sweden and France by stages, reduced much of central Europe to ruins, and cost the German lands especially dearly in population.

The Peace of Westphalia of 1648 ended the war. It reshaped imperial constitutional arrangements and European diplomacy, but did not create a modern system of equal sovereign states in one act; treating 1648 as that system’s birth is a contested later narrative.

Main settlement Meaning Later influence
Imperial constitutional adjustment Confirmed rights of imperial estates within a continuing empire Not universal modern sovereign equality
Confession follows territory Catholicism, Lutheranism and Calvinism recognized as co-existing Religious pluralism institutionalized in central Europe
Conflict managed by treaty Multilateral negotiation over religious and political disputes A precedent for diplomacy and the balance-of-power system

Note that the Ottomans took no part in this "European war" — they were occupied with their confrontation with the Habsburgs in Hungary and with Venice over Crete. European politics never ran on a single board.

This stage can be summarized as: the institutional unity of Latin Christendom was shaken; new arrangements took shape among confessions, dynasties and polities; and at the same time southeastern Europe was drawn into the timetable of another empire. State formation remained a long process, and religious authority did not disappear from politics.
