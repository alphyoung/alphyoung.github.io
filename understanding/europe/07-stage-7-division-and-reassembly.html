07 Stage 7 Division and Reassembly

1945 — Present

After the Second World War, half of Europe was rebuilding and half was being remade. The Cold War cut the continent into two, each running different institutions, economies and military blocs. The upheavals of 1989 made that dividing line void overnight, but they did not produce a tidy Europe — the old fissures were replaced by new tensions.

  • 1945 Second World War ends
  • 1947 Marshall Plan
  • 1949 NATO founded
  • 1955 Warsaw Pact founded
  • 1961 Berlin Wall
  • 1968 Prague Spring
  • 1989 Revolutions in Eastern Europe; the Berlin Wall falls
  • 1991 USSR dissolves
  • 1993 European Union founded

7.1 Cut in two

1945 — 1955

Wartime alliance quickly turned into confrontation. The Western bloc centred on the United States rebuilt Western Europe's economy through the Marshall Plan (proposed in 1947, implemented from 1948) and founded NATO in 1949; the Soviet Union built a sphere of influence in Eastern Europe, set up the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance in 1949, and organized the Warsaw Pact in 1955. The "Iron Curtain" ran from the Baltic to the Adriatic, and Europe was cut in two.

The symbol of that division was Berlin. The Soviet Union blockaded West Berlin in 1948–1949 and the West supplied it by airlift; by the time the Berlin Wall was built in 1961, the same city had been physically separated by walls, a guarded strip and watchtowers, each side running a different system.

The developmental paths on either side of the line were vastly different. Western Europe recovered rapidly through American aid and internal market integration, with welfare states gradually taking shape; Eastern Europe, following the Soviet model, pressed ahead with nationalization, collective farms and five-year plans, giving priority to heavy industry and enduring chronic shortages of consumer goods. This dividing line was not natural; it was drawn by the outcome of the war.

7.2 East of the Iron Curtain: bloc-building and its cracks

1948 — 1989

Transplanting the Soviet model did not always go smoothly, and eastern European history contains several recurring cracks:

  • After the February 1948 events in Czechoslovakia, non-communist forces were excluded from government, and one-party dominance was established across the bloc.
  • Popular protests broke out in East Berlin in 1953, and in Poznań and Hungary in 1956. During the Hungarian events the Nagy government briefly announced withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact and a multi-party system, and was then suppressed by Soviet force.
  • The Prague Spring of 1968 attempted "socialism with a human face" and was ended that August by an invasion of Warsaw Pact troops. Afterwards the Soviet Union advanced the "limited sovereignty" doctrine, making clear that bloc members could not leave.
  • In 1980 workers at the Gdańsk shipyard in Poland founded Solidarity, which at its height had more than ten million members; martial law was imposed in 1981 and the union went underground, but it never disappeared.

Meanwhile eastern European societies were changing quietly: literacy and industrialization rose sharply and urbanization advanced, but the consumer gap with Western Europe widened steadily, and restrictions on travel and information became a lasting source of resentment. The legitimacy of the system came to rest more and more on economic performance, and economic performance grew worse and worse.

"Eastern Europe" was redefined during the Cold War. It turned from a geographical direction into the name of a political bloc. This also explains why Poles and Czechs so often insist that they belong to "central Europe" — what they want to escape is that political label, not their geographical position.

7.3 Western Europe: reconstruction and one cooperative arrangement

1945 — 1993

In the institutional history of Western Europe in this period there is one development often narrated as the main line of European history: regional integration. It is worth knowing about, but it needs putting back in its real place — this was an arrangement among some Western European states under particular conditions, not a common direction for all of Europe.

In 1951 France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg signed the Treaty of Paris, and the following year established the European Coal and Steel Community, placing the resources of war under common management. In 1957 the six signed the Treaty of Rome, and in 1958 the European Economic Community came into being, moving step by step toward a customs union and a common market. In 1993 the Treaty on European Union (Maastricht) entered into force and the European Union was formally established; from 1999 the euro was used as a unit of account, and from 2002 it circulated.

Its boundaries must be remembered at the same time: from the outset it covered only part of Western Europe — Norway, Switzerland, Austria, Sweden and Finland long remained outside; the United Kingdom did not join until 1973; and the whole of Eastern Europe then belonged to another bloc. Nor was it ever a "United States of Europe": member states kept their armies, diplomacy and taxation, pooling only part of their economic and regulatory sovereignty.

Why did integration leave Eastern Europe out? Because for Western European politicians of the 1950s the first problem was preventing another war between France and Germany, and rebuilding their economies under American protection. Eastern Europe was then neither at their negotiating table nor within their security framework. To read the EU as "the unification of Europe" conceals this fact.

7.4 1989: upheaval and reassembly

1989 — 1991

Gorbachev's reforms from 1985 loosened Soviet control over Eastern Europe. In 1989 the upheavals came one after another: Poland held semi-free elections, Hungary opened its border with Austria, Czechoslovakia had its "Velvet Revolution", and Romania's regime changed amid violence. On 9 November the Berlin Wall was opened; on 3 October 1990 the two Germanys were united.

At the end of 1991 the Soviet Union dissolved. The Cold War ended without a direct full-scale war between the two superpowers. The countries of central and eastern Europe turned toward multi-party politics and market economies, and applied in succession to join the EU and NATO — this time it was they who asked to "return to Europe."

But reassembly was not entirely peaceful. Yugoslavia's disintegration produced the most brutal war in Europe since 1945: Slovenia and Croatia in 1991, the Bosnian war of 1992–1995, the Kosovo war of 1999, with the Srebrenica massacre and large-scale "ethnic cleansing" in between. In the same period Czechoslovakia divided peacefully into two states in 1993. The national question did not disappear with the Cold War; it had merely been held down by the confrontation of the two blocs.

7.5 Europe since 2000

1991 — Present

At the turn of the century the European picture showed division and reassembly happening at once:

  • Some central and eastern European states joined NATO first; in 2004 ten more countries joined the EU (including Malta and Cyprus), followed by Bulgaria and Romania in 2007 and Croatia in 2013.
  • Membership brought funding, markets and mobility — and new disparities too: populations in the east have moved west, while doubts about immigration and integration have grown in the west.
  • In 2020 the United Kingdom left the EU — integration is not irreversible.

The questions facing Europe today are mostly not about "deepening integration" but about internal tensions and conflicts on its periphery: the relationship between sovereignty and union, welfare and an ageing population, migration and identity, energy and security. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 brought large-scale war back to the European continent and led the Nordic and eastern European states to reassess their security positions; Finland and Sweden subsequently applied to join NATO.

Looking back, the EU is only one of many present-day European arrangements: it does not cover the whole of Europe, it does not represent a common direction for all countries, and it cannot explain what is happening in the Balkans, the Caucasus or Ukraine. Europe has never been a frozen entity, but a process of continual reassembly — reassembled sometimes by dynasties and churches, sometimes by wars and treaties, sometimes by trade and the movement of people.
