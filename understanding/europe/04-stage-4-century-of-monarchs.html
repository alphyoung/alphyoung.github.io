04 Stage 4 Century of Monarchs

After the Thirty Years' War European polities continued to reshape their arrangements, and stronger fiscal and military capacity became an important goal for several monarchies: absolute monarchs institutionalized administration, taxation and standing armies. At the same time more violent changes were under way in the east of Europe — three new great powers rose between the Baltic and the Black Sea, a state several centuries old vanished from the map, and in the southeast the Ottoman Empire shifted from attack to defence.

4.1 Absolute monarchy and "reason of state"

1648 — 1750

After the chaos of the Thirty Years' War, "the strong state" became the fashionable answer. Louis XIV of France domesticated the nobility at Versailles and built an absolute monarchy administered directly by central officials (he is said to have declared, "I am the state" — though the line may be a later attribution, it captures his conception of rule). He revoked the Edict of Nantes, suppressed Protestantism, and brought religion too under state management.

Imitators followed in quick succession: the Hohenzollerns in Prussia, the Habsburgs in Austria, Peter the Great in Russia — all building standing armies, unified tax systems and bureaucracies. "Reason of state", which took the interest of the state as the highest criterion, gradually replaced religion as the declared cause of war.

The political logic of the period was to gather dispersed feudal rights into the monarch's hands; and the problem it left behind was equally stark — estates, local privileges and parliaments could also constrain rulers. England’s Revolution of 1688–1689 and Bill of Rights strengthened parliamentary limits on monarchy, providing an important alternative to absolutism.

"Absolute" did not mean "all-powerful". Even in Louis XIV's France the monarch still faced provincial estates, parlements, regional privileges and the entrenched interests of the Church. Absolute monarchy was better described as a two-century effort at centralization than as an accomplished reality. Eastern monarchs, by contrast, typically faced weaker towns and stronger nobility — which is why their route to centralization looked quite different.

4.2 In the east: three new powers and one vanished state

1700 — 1800

While Western Europe competed over colonies and sea power, the east of Europe was being redrawn.

Russia changed most of all. Peter the Great (r. 1682–1725) pushed through westernization in an almost brutal manner: moving the capital to St Petersburg, founding a navy, reforming administration and the army, forcing the nobility to change how they lived. The Great Northern War of 1700–1721 defeated Sweden and won an outlet on the Baltic; in 1721 Russia formally took the title of empire. Catherine II then continued to expand south and west, and Russia grew from a corner of eastern Europe into a colossus straddling Europe and Asia.

Prussia grew from a marginal electorate into a military power through Frederick William I's harsh management of army and finances, and through the Silesian wars launched by Frederick II after 1740. Austria, though set against Prussia within Germany, ruled through the Habsburg family over a multinational composite including Hungary, Bohemia, the southern Netherlands and parts of Italy — less a nation-state than a dynastic patchwork.

The price was paid by Poland–Lithuania. A state that had accommodated many peoples and confessions, and in 1791 adopted one of Europe's earlier modern written constitutions, was long paralysed by the "liberum veto" of its noble republic. In 1772 Russia, Prussia and Austria carried out the first partition of Poland; a second followed in 1793; after the third partition in 1795 Poland–Lithuania disappeared from the map, and did not return until 1918.

Russia Forced westernization and military expansion under the tsar Serfdom retained for a long time; expansion to the Black Sea and the Baltic

When did the term "Eastern Europe" appear? To a large extent it was constructed after this period. Enlightenment-era Western observers commonly described the east as "backward", "despotic" and "agricultural", the better to contrast it with their own "progress". In fact Poland's noble republic and religious toleration were once more "free" than most Western states, while Russia's and Prussia's capacity for a strong state made Western monarchs envious. To treat the region as a homogeneous backwater is classic hindsight.

4.3 The Ottoman retreat

1683 — 1800

In the summer of 1683 Ottoman forces besieged Vienna for the second time. On this occasion the Polish king Jan III Sobieski led a relief army that joined with the Habsburgs and German princes to drive the besiegers off, a turning point in the central European war. The counter-offensive that followed over the next decade ended with the Treaty of Karlowitz in 1699: the Ottomans ceded European territory on a large scale for the first time, and Hungary and Transylvania passed to the Habsburgs.

The retreat continued through the eighteenth century. The Treaty of Passarowitz in 1718 lost further territory; the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774 gave Russia an outlet on the Black Sea and a claim to "protect" Orthodox subjects within the Ottoman Empire — a clause that laid the fuse for the "Eastern Question" of the following century; in 1783 Russia annexed the Crimean Khanate.

Year Event or treaty Change
1683 Siege of Vienna repulsed Expansion in central Europe halted
1699 Treaty of Karlowitz Hungary and Transylvania to the Habsburgs
1718 Treaty of Passarowitz Northern Serbia and parts of Wallachia change hands
1774 Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca Russia gains a Black Sea outlet and a protectorate over Orthodox Christians

For Balkan Christians this period was deeply contradictory: the Ottoman order was loosening, but what replaced it was not a local independent state so much as another empire (the Habsburgs) or another "protector" (Russia). The seeds of national awakening were sown in that disappointment.

4.4 The scientific revolution: from Copernicus to Newton

1543 — 1687

Alongside the concentration of monarchical power came a revolution in how the world was seen. This thread runs across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and finally changed the way human beings understood nature. Copernicus proposed heliocentrism in 1543, shaking the Earth-centred picture of the cosmos; Galileo observed Jupiter’s moons in 1610 and was tried in 1633 over his defence of the Earth’s motion and the restrictions imposed in 1616.

In 1687 Newton published the Principia Mathematica, bringing heavens and earth into a single mathematical system through the laws of motion and universal gravitation. Thereafter the universe was understood as a machine that could be calculated and predicted.

The revolution's deeper influence went beyond astronomy. It established a new source of authority: no longer texts and doctrine, but observation, experiment and reasoning. "Natural law" became a general framework for explaining the world — and the method would soon be applied to society and politics.

Science and religion were not simply opposed. Copernicus, Kepler and Newton were themselves largely religious men, and much of the impetus of early modern science came from "understanding the design of the Creator". What was actually broken was the Church's monopoly on explaining nature, not faith itself.

4.5 The Enlightenment

1687 — 1789

If nature follows laws, might society follow laws too? Eighteenth-century thinkers carried the method and the confidence of science over to human affairs. Locke wrote of natural rights and limited government, Montesquieu of the separation of powers, Voltaire attacked clerical power and arbitrary rule, Rousseau proposed popular sovereignty. The Encyclopédie edited by Diderot gathered the new knowledge into volumes and spread it widely.

The core demand of the Enlightenment was to move the ground of judgement from tradition and authority to public reason and discussion. It asserted universal human rights and the rule of law, and required room for speech and publication.

Yet the Enlightenment was born in an age of expanding empire and slavery. The contradiction runs through it: the same slogans could be used to demand equality and to justify domination.

How did the Enlightenment see the east? Some thinkers treated the Ottoman Empire or Russia as specimens of "enlightened despotism"; others treated them as Europe's cautionary counter-example. The seeds of the nineteenth-century language of "civilized versus barbarian" were already planted here. Understanding European history also means understanding how Europe imagined the Europe beyond itself.

4.6 Global rivalry: mercantilism and colonial competition

1650 — 1789

The balance of power among sovereign states quickly extended overseas. In the seventeenth century the Dutch were pre-eminent through shipping and finance; the English then displaced them through a series of navigation acts and wars. By the eighteenth century Britain and France were the protagonists of global competition.

The guiding economic doctrine was mercantilism: bullion and a trade surplus as the measure of national strength, colonies as sources of raw material and markets. This zero-sum thinking bound colonial expansion tightly to interstate war. The Seven Years' War of 1756–1763 is often called a rehearsal for a world war: its theatres stretched from Europe to North America and India, and afterwards Britain held the advantage at sea and in the colonies.

But the costs accumulated too: taxes levied to share the expense of war eventually inflamed resistance in the North American colonies. In 1776 the United States declared independence — Enlightenment political ideas became important resources for building a new nation.
