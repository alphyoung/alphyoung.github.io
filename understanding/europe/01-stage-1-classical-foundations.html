01 Stage 1 Classical Foundations

Europe's three sources — the Greek city-state and its thought, Roman law and unity, Christian faith and organization — met and merged along the Mediterranean. But this foundation was never one undivided block: when the Western imperial framework came apart in 476, the Eastern Roman Empire lasted nearly a thousand years more. From then on European history would always run along two lines at once.

  • 776 BC First recorded Olympic Games (traditional date)
  • 509 BC Roman Republic
  • 27 BC Roman Empire

1.1 Greece: the city-state, the citizen and the beginning of "Europe"

800 — 146 BC

Europe's earliest self-awareness appeared among the small, mutually independent communities along the Aegean coast. The Greeks called them the polis — not a stretch of territory but a political unit constituted by a community of citizens. Athens, Sparta and Corinth each governed themselves; they shared a language and a pantheon, but no common political authority.

In the fifth century BC Persia invaded twice, and the Greek cities prevailed at Marathon (490 BC) and Salamis (480 BC). Later ages endlessly invoked that victory as the archetype of "Europe" against "the East" — although no political identity called "Europe" existed at the time. What truly drained the city-states was their own war: the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC), which ended in Athens’ defeat and Spartan hegemony, while prolonged warfare weakened the Greek city-state world.

Athens' legacy lay not in territory but in ideas: the assembly of citizens, the jury court, tragedy and philosophy turned "public affairs" into something that could be argued about. It was also limited — women, foreigners and slaves were excluded from citizenship.

Where does the word "Europe" come from?

Europa was originally a Phoenician princess in Greek myth, and only gradually came to denote the landmass west of the Aegean. "Europe" thus began as a vague geographical and cultural direction, not a unified entity. To treat it as an age-old whole is hindsight.

Territory · the Hellenistic world — In 336 BC Alexander of Macedon came to the throne and in 334 BC launched his eastern campaign, reaching the Indus valley within little more than a decade. After his death the empire split into the Hellenistic kingdoms of the Ptolemies, the Seleucids and Macedon, stretching from Egypt to Central Asia. "Greece" was by then no longer a city-state but a cultural sphere spanning three continents.

1.2 Rome: from republic to empire

509 BC — AD 284

Rome began as a single town on the Tiber. In 509 BC, according to tradition, the kings were expelled and a republic was founded, with the Senate, the consuls and the citizen assemblies forming a complex balance of powers. Legions and a system of alliances absorbed Italy step by step, and the Punic Wars (264–146 BC) destroyed Carthage and turned the Mediterranean into "our sea".

Expansion also brought imbalance. Widening inequality and the privatization of armies gradually disabled the republic's checks and balances. Caesar crossed the Rubicon (49 BC) and was then assassinated (44 BC); by 27 BC, when Octavian received the title "Augustus", the principate was established and Rome entered the imperial age. The following two centuries are known as the Pax Romana, when roads, law and trade bound the Mediterranean world into one.

Dimension Republic (509–27 BC) Empire (after 27 BC)
Centre of power Senate and annually elected consuls The princeps (emperor) controls the army and the provinces
Source of legitimacy Citizen elections and tradition Army acclamation plus titles granted by the Senate
Mode of expansion Alliances and provinces alongside sustained conquest Direct rule centered on the emperor
Legacy The political vocabulary of republicanism Roman law, Latin, provincial administration

Rome's most enduring legacy was law. From the Twelve Tables to the juristic compilations of the later empire, a body of rules on property, contract and status was repeatedly refined — and became the common source of the continental civil-law tradition.

1.3 Christianity: from persecuted sect to state religion

AD 30 — 392

Christianity was born in Judaea under Roman rule, at first one small sect among the empire's many religions, long excluded and persecuted. The turn came with the Edict of Milan in 313: following Galerius’ toleration edict of 311, Constantine and Licinius reaffirmed religious toleration in 313 and provided for the restitution of Christian property. Thereafter it moved rapidly toward imperial power.

The Council of Nicaea in 325 sought to settle theological disputes — and set the precedent of an emperor intervening in church affairs. In 380 the Edict of Thessalonica established imperial support for Nicene Christianity, rather than for all Christian confessions indiscriminately. Meanwhile, in 330 Constantine moved his capital to Constantinople on the Bosphorus, shifting the empire's centre of gravity eastward.

An easily overlooked fact: Christianity spread across Asia, Europe and Africa. Egypt, Syria and Asia Minor were for a long time more thoroughly "Christianized" than Western Europe. To equate Christianity simply with "the religion of Europe" obscures its far wider Mediterranean setting.

1.4 Two roads: the end of the West and the survival of the East

284 — 476

The empire was too large for one man to govern alone. Diocletian came to power in 284 and in 293 created the Tetrarchy; on Theodosius' death in 395 the empire was ruled by two emperors, one in the East and one in the West, and that administrative separation endured. The West suffered chronic financial weakness, filled its armies with Germanic commanders, and faced mounting pressure on the frontier.

In 476 the Germanic commander Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, the last Western emperor ruling in Italy, and the event became the conventional endpoint of the Western Roman Empire; Julius Nepos nevertheless retained the imperial title in Dalmatia until 480. Yet the year drew little notice at the time. The real change was this: the West was replaced by a patchwork of Germanic kingdoms, and a "Roman Mediterranean world" dissolved.

But that is only half the story. The Eastern Roman Empire did not end. Centred on Constantinople, using Greek as its administrative language, carrying Roman law forward through the Code of Justinian and Christianity through the Orthodox Church, it remained the strongest political body in the eastern Mediterranean until 1453. Later ages called it the Byzantine Empire — a name invented by sixteenth-century scholars; the people themselves always called themselves Romans.

Aspect The West (Latin) The East (Greek)
Language Latin, later diverging into the Romance languages Greek, which kept official status throughout
Church The bishop of Rome, gradually becoming the papacy The patriarch of Constantinople, closely tied to the throne
Politics Many Germanic kingdoms, power dispersed Centralized imperial power, bureaucracy and taxation intact
Fate Imperial office lapses in 476 Continues until 1453

Please do not read 476 as "civilization died." More accurately: the imperial administrative framework in the West collapsed, the Eastern Empire survived for nearly a thousand years more, and three forces — the Germanic peoples, the old Roman aristocracy and the Christian Church — were recombining into Latin medieval Europe. The eastern half took another road, and its line — Byzantium, Orthodoxy, the Slavic world — will keep returning in every chapter that follows.
