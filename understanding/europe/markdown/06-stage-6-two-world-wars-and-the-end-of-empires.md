# 06 Stage 6 Two World Wars and the End of Empires

1914 — 1945

Two world wars not only pushed Europe from the centre of the world into ruin; they also ended the imperial system that had ruled this continent for centuries: the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman empires collapsed one after another after the First World War, and the vacuum they left was crowded with newborn nation-states, unsettled borders and mutually hostile minorities. The Second World War redrew Europe more thoroughly still — including its population.

* **1914** First World War begins
* **1917** Russian Revolution
* **1919** Treaty of Versailles
* **1923** Republic of Turkey founded
* **1939** Second World War begins
* **1945** Second World War ends

## 6.1 The First World War: total war and the collapse of four empires

1914 — 1923

The **First World War** of 1914–1918 was Europe's first industrialized total war: trenches, machine guns, gas and artillery consumed an entire generation. Its theatres ran from Flanders on the Western Front to Galicia in the east, the Isonzo in the south, and Mesopotamia and Palestine under Ottoman rule.

**By the war's end four empires no longer existed.** The German Empire fell in the November Revolution of 1918 and the Hohenzollerns abdicated; Austria-Hungary disintegrated at the same time, the last Habsburg emperor relinquishing power; the Russian Empire had already been overthrown in the **February and October revolutions of 1917**, ending the tsarist system, with the Bolsheviks establishing Soviet power and concluding a separate peace with Germany in 1918; the **Ottoman Empire** signed an armistice in 1918, the sultanate was abolished in 1922, **the Republic of Turkey was founded in 1923**, and the caliphate itself was abolished in 1924.

| Empire            | How it ended                       | What followed                                                |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| German Empire     | Revolution of 1918; the monarch abdicates | Weimar Republic → later the Nazi seizure of power       |
| Austria-Hungary   | Disintegration in 1918; nations separate | Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and others |
| Russian Empire    | Revolution of 1917; tsarism ends   | Soviet Russia → the USSR                                     |
| Ottoman Empire    | Armistice in 1918; sultanate abolished 1922 | Republic of Turkey; Middle Eastern and Balkan territories divided |

{% hint style="info" %}
**These four collapses produced very different outcomes.** In Germany and Austria-Hungary the fall of the old dynasty brought republics and parliamentary politics; in Russia it brought one-party rule and a planned economy; in the Ottoman core it brought a thorough reconstruction from empire to nation-state. **One year, four completely different endings** — which is why 1918 should not be read as a single "common rebirth of Europe".
{% endhint %}

## 6.2 The Versailles system and the new map of Europe

1919 — 1929

The **Treaty of Versailles** of 1919 imposed territorial cessions, armament limits and heavy reparations on a defeated Germany, and included the Covenant establishing the **League of Nations**, which formally came into existence on 10 January 1920. Article 231 required Germany to accept responsibility for the losses and damage attributed to Germany and its allies, as a basis for reparations. The wider post-war settlement had limited enforcement and mediation capacity. The Weimar Republic struggled through inflation and humiliation, and the **Great Depression** of 1929 made matters worse.

On the ruins of empire, **a whole row of new or restored states appeared in central and eastern Europe**: Poland was restored in 1918 (after more than a hundred and twenty years of partition), Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia (first called the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes) were founded, Hungary and Austria separated from the dual monarchy, Romania greatly expanded, and the three Baltic states became independent. Almost all of them faced two common difficulties:

* **Borders and populations did not coincide** — the new frontiers often split one people between two states, and enclosed minorities within the new states.
* **Economies and security were fragile** — new tariff walls cut the old imperial markets apart; they feared Germany and feared the Soviet Union, and frequently quarrelled with one another over territory.

The limits of the Versailles settlement were not simply that it was "too harsh" or "too lenient" towards Germany, but that **it handed a new map to mutually suspicious states in a world with no real arbiter**.

## 6.3 Totalitarianism and the Second World War

1919 — 1945

Post-war crises and political conflicts fostered different dictatorships. Mussolini became Italy’s prime minister in 1922, before the Great Depression. The crisis after 1929 deepened Germany’s political instability and aided Nazism; Stalin’s system emerged from the Soviet revolutionary and party-state context. Hitler came to power in 1933 and tore up the Versailles settlement step by step, annexing Austria and the Sudetenland in 1938, then occupying the Czech lands as a protectorate in 1939 while Slovakia became a German-dependent state. **In September 1939 Germany invaded Poland**, and Britain and France then declared war on Germany, beginning the war in Europe.

This was a genuinely global war, and the scene of the most terrible atrocities in human history: its theatres covered Europe, North Africa and Asia-Pacific, and Nazi Germany and its allies and collaborators systematically murdered approximately six million Jews in the **Holocaust**, while also persecuting and murdering large numbers of other victims. Germany surrendered unconditionally in 1945, and Europe set out again from amid ruins and moral shock.

## 6.4 Eastern Europe in the Second World War

1939 — 1945

This war took a very different form in central and eastern Europe: it was not only a war between armies, but **occupation, forced labour, deportation and extermination**.

In August 1939 Germany and the Soviet Union signed a non-aggression treaty that divided spheres of influence, and in September they **partitioned Poland together**. In 1941 Germany launched **Operation Barbarossa** against the Soviet Union, and central and eastern Europe became the cruellest section of the front: Leningrad was besieged for nearly nine hundred days, Warsaw was levelled after the 1944 uprising, and Belarus and Ukraine lost an appalling proportion of their populations as the two sides surged back and forth.

**The principal sites of the Holocaust were also in eastern Europe.** The extermination camps at Auschwitz, Treblinka, Sobibór and Bełżec were built in occupied Poland; Jews were transported there from western, central and southeastern Europe. Roma, Soviet prisoners of war and civilians in occupied territories were murdered on a mass scale as well. **The demographic and urban losses suffered in what is today called "Eastern Europe" between 1939 and 1945 are the key to understanding the region's memory and politics in the second half of the twentieth century.**

By 1945 something else of lasting consequence had happened in central and eastern Europe: **an overall westward shift of borders and populations**. Poland's eastern territories were incorporated into the Soviet Union, and Germany's eastern territories were assigned to Poland; millions of Germans were expelled from Czechoslovakia, Poland and Hungary, while Poles and Ukrainians were resettled in what were called "population exchanges". **Europe's map and its distribution of population were rearranged in 1945 according to the will of the victors.**

{% hint style="info" %}
**The "post-war order" had two faces.** In Western Europe it meant the Marshall Plan and the beginnings of regional cooperation; in central and eastern Europe it meant being assigned to the Soviet sphere, having borders rewritten and institutions replaced. The end of the same war was a completely different beginning for different parts of Europe — which is the subject of the next chapter.
{% endhint %}
