# 01 Stage 1 Classical Foundations

Europe's three sources — the Greek city-state and its thought, Roman law and unity, Christian faith and organization — met and merged within the framework of the Roman Empire. The end of the Western Empire in 476 was not a rupture in civilization, but the passage of this foundation into the Middle Ages.

* **776 BC** First Olympic Games
* **509 BC** Roman Republic
* **27 BC** Roman Empire
* **313** Edict of Milan
* **476** Western Rome falls

## 1.1 Greece: the city-state, the citizen and the beginning of "Europe"

800 — 146 BC

Europe's earliest self-awareness appeared among the small, mutually independent communities along the Aegean coast. The Greeks called them the **polis** — not a stretch of territory but a political unit constituted by a community of citizens. Athens, Sparta and Corinth each governed themselves; they shared a language and a pantheon, but no common political authority.

In the fifth century BC Persia invaded twice, and the Greek cities prevailed at **Marathon (490 BC)** and **Salamis (480 BC)**. Later ages endlessly invoked that victory as the archetype of "Europe" against "the East" — although no political identity called "Europe" existed at the time. What truly drained the city-states was their own war: the **Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC)**, which left Athens and Sparta both broken.

Athens' legacy lay not in territory but in ideas: the assembly of citizens, the jury court, tragedy and philosophy turned "public affairs" into something that could be argued about. It was also limited — women, foreigners and slaves were excluded from citizenship.

### Where does the word "Europe" come from?

Europa was originally a Phoenician princess in Greek myth, and only gradually came to denote the landmass west of the Aegean. "Europe" thus began as a vague geographical and cultural direction, not a unified entity. To treat it as an age-old whole is hindsight.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Territory · the Hellenistic world** — In 336 BC Alexander of Macedon came to the throne and in 334 BC launched his eastern campaign, reaching the Indus valley within little more than a decade. After his death the empire split into the Hellenistic kingdoms of the Ptolemies, the Seleucids and Macedon, stretching from Egypt to Central Asia. "Greece" was by then no longer a city-state but a cultural sphere spanning three continents.
{% endhint %}

## 1.2 Rome: from republic to empire

509 BC — AD 284

Rome began as a single town on the Tiber. In 509 BC, according to tradition, the kings were expelled and a **republic** was founded, with the Senate, the consuls and the citizen assemblies forming a complex balance of powers. Legions and a system of alliances absorbed Italy step by step, and the **Punic Wars (264–146 BC)** destroyed Carthage and turned the Mediterranean into "our sea".

Expansion also brought imbalance. Widening inequality and the privatization of armies gradually disabled the republic's checks and balances. Caesar crossed the Rubicon (49 BC) and was then assassinated (44 BC); by **27 BC, when Octavian received the title "Augustus"**, the principate was established and Rome entered the imperial age. The following two centuries are known as the **Pax Romana**, when roads, law and trade bound the Mediterranean world into one.

| Dimension            | Republic (509–27 BC)                                     | Empire (after 27 BC)                                       |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Centre of power      | Senate and annually elected consuls                      | The princeps (emperor) controls the army and the provinces |
| Source of legitimacy | Citizen elections and tradition                          | Army acclamation plus titles granted by the Senate         |
| Mode of expansion    | Allies and provinces side by side, relatively restrained | Direct rule centered on the emperor                        |
| Legacy               | The political vocabulary of republicanism                | Roman law, Latin, provincial administration                |

Rome's most enduring legacy was **law**. From the Twelve Tables to the juristic compilations of the later empire, a body of rules on property, contract and status was repeatedly refined — and became the common source of the continental civil-law tradition.

## 1.3 Christianity: from persecuted sect to state religion

AD 30 — 392

Christianity was born in Judaea under Roman rule, at first one small sect among the empire's many religions, long excluded and persecuted. The turn came with the **Edict of Milan in 313**: Constantine and Licinius proclaimed freedom of belief, and Christianity gained legal standing. Thereafter it moved rapidly toward imperial power.

The **Council of Nicaea in 325** sought to settle theological disputes — and set the precedent of an emperor intervening in church affairs. In 380 the **Edict of Thessalonica** made Christianity the state religion. Meanwhile, in 330 Constantine moved his capital to **Constantinople** on the Bosphorus, shifting the empire's centre of gravity eastward.

{% hint style="info" %}
**An easily overlooked fact:** Christianity spread across Asia, Europe and Africa. Egypt, Syria and Asia Minor were for a long time more thoroughly "Christianized" than Western Europe. To equate Christianity simply with "the religion of Europe" obscures its far wider Mediterranean setting.
{% endhint %}

## 1.4 476: the end of the Western Roman Empire

284 — 476

The empire was too large for one man to govern alone. Diocletian came to power in 284 and in 293 created the **Tetrarchy**; on Theodosius' death in 395 the empire was formally divided into East and West and never reunited. The West suffered chronic financial weakness, filled its armies with Germanic commanders, and faced mounting pressure on the frontier.

**In 476 the Germanic commander Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, the last Western emperor**, and the Western Roman Empire nominally came to an end. Yet the year drew little notice at the time — the Eastern (Byzantine) Empire continued, and church, law and urban life did not vanish overnight. The real change was this: the West was replaced by a patchwork of Germanic kingdoms, and a "Roman Mediterranean world" dissolved.

{% hint style="warning" %}
**Please do not read 476 as "civilization died."** More accurately: the imperial administrative framework in the West collapsed, the Eastern Empire survived for nearly a thousand years more, and three forces — the Germanic peoples, the old Roman aristocracy and the Christian Church — were recombining into medieval Europe.
{% endhint %}
