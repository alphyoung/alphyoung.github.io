# 05 Stage 5 Revolution, Nation and Empire

The French Revolution pushed "sovereignty of the people" into the centre of politics, and Napoleon's wars carried the idea across Europe; the Vienna system tried to restore order but could not hold back nationalism and industrialization. Yet the political forms of this period were far more than one: to the west, nation-states; to the east, multinational dynastic empires; to the southeast, an Ottoman Empire in the process of losing its European territory. **Nationalism was both a force for building states and a force for dismantling them.**

* **1789** French Revolution
* **1815** Congress of Vienna
* **1830** Greek independence
* **1848** Year of Revolutions
* **1861 / 1871** Italy and Germany unified
* **1878** Congress of Berlin
* **1912–1913** Balkan Wars
* **1914** First World War

## 5.1 The French Revolution and Napoleon

1789 — 1815

Financial crisis forced Louis XVI to convene the Estates-General, and the gates swung open unexpectedly. **In 1789** the Third Estate formed itself into a National Assembly, the Bastille was stormed, and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen proclaimed popular sovereignty and equality before the law. The Revolution then grew steadily more radical: the republic founded in 1792, the king executed in 1793, and the **Terror** imposed thereafter, and then a return to conservatism through the Thermidorian reaction.

In 1799 **Napoleon Bonaparte** took power, and in 1804 had himself crowned emperor. With the Napoleonic Code he institutionalized part of the Revolution's achievement — equality before the law, private property, freedom of contract — and carried it across Europe with his armies. But his conquests also provoked national resistance in the occupied lands, and ended in total defeat at **Waterloo** in 1815.

| Phase                   | Dates       | Characteristics                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Constitutional monarchy | 1789 — 1792 | Constitution, abolition of feudal privilege, Declaration of Rights |
| Republic and Terror     | 1792 — 1795 | King executed, foreign war, Jacobin dictatorship                   |
| Directory               | 1795 — 1799 | Turbulence and unstable politics                                   |
| Napoleonic era          | 1799 — 1815 | The Code, conquest, hegemony, final defeat                         |

{% hint style="info" %}
**Napoleon also left his mark in the east.** In 1798 he campaigned in Egypt; militarily it came to nothing, but it broke the Ottoman Empire's direct control over Egypt and allowed local powers to grow. Europe's great powers now saw more clearly that **Ottoman territory could be contested** — this is where what was later called the "Eastern Question" begins.
{% endhint %}

## 5.2 The Vienna system and nationalism

1815 — 1848

In 1814–1815 the victors redrew Europe at the **Congress of Vienna**. Its manager **Metternich** pursued "equilibrium" and "legitimacy": restoring the dynasties Napoleon had overturned, suppressing revolution and national claims, and maintaining order through great-power diplomacy, which cannot be reduced to the Holy Alliance of Russia, Prussia and Austria; Britain and, later, France were also central. The system kept the peace in outline for nearly forty years.

But it had an inner difficulty: the Congress treated **national claims** as a disturbance to be suppressed. Poland was partitioned again, Germany and Italy remained divided, and within the Habsburg Empire lived Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Croats, Italians… **The idea of "one people, one state" meant, in these places, the dissolution of empire.**

In 1848 revolution swept Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Milan and Budapest — the **Year of Revolutions**. Most of the revolutions were put down, yet they nailed the questions of nationhood and constitutional government firmly onto Europe's agenda.

{% hint style="info" %}
**1848 in eastern Europe: a different revolution.** In Hungary, the movement led by Kossuth briefly established an independent government and army and in 1849 declared the Habsburg dynasty deposed; it was finally crushed by Austrian forces acting with Russian help. In Bohemia, delegates of the Slavic peoples met in Prague to demand national equality within the empire. **These demands were not the same as the liberal parliamentary movement of the West** — what they had to settle was "who counts as a political subject inside a multinational empire".
{% endhint %}

## 5.3 The Industrial Revolution and social change

1760 — 1900

In late eighteenth-century Britain, mechanized textiles and the factory system developed alongside increasing use of coal, iron and steam power; steam railways expanded chiefly in the nineteenth century. Industrialization spread from Britain to Belgium, France and Germany, turning Europe into "the workshop of the world". Cities swelled enormously, and population structure, family forms and the sense of time were all reshaped.

The costs were enormous as well: workers laboured long hours in grim conditions, child labour was widespread, and inequality widened. In response came trade unions, cooperatives and socialist thought. The **Communist Manifesto of 1848**, the later First International and the national social-democratic parties were all political products of that contradiction. Industrialization also vastly increased the military and economic capacity of states, setting the stage for the total wars to come.

{% hint style="info" %}
**The map of industrialization was very uneven.** By the mid-nineteenth century Britain, Belgium and western Germany were thick with chimneys, while Russia, the Balkans and most of Iberia remained agricultural; Russia did not abolish serfdom until 1861. **That gap later turned directly into a political gap**: industrial powers could produce machine guns and railway networks, while agrarian empires could only hold on through manpower and grain.
{% endhint %}

## 5.4 National unification: Italy and Germany

1848 — 1871

The clearest cases of nationalism turning from an idea into fact are Italy and Germany. Italy had long been divided into many states, some of them under Austrian control. Through **Cavour**'s diplomacy and **Garibaldi**'s campaigns, the **Kingdom of Italy** was proclaimed in 1861; Rome was recovered in 1870 and became the capital in 1871, completing unification.

Germany advanced along the "Lesser German" line, excluding Austria. **Bismarck**, known for "blood and iron", cleared the ground through three wars — against Denmark, Austria and France. **In 1871 the German Empire was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles**, with Wilhelm I as emperor. A new great power had appeared in the middle of Europe, and the balance of power changed with it.

Prussia → North German Confederation → German Empire (1871)

The manner of unification planted trouble too: the German crown was proclaimed on French soil, leaving a long Franco-German grievance; and the new empire's industry and military strength grew very fast, with no matching international arrangement to accommodate them.

## 5.5 The imperial frontier: Greece, the Balkans and the "Eastern Question"

1821 — 1913

While Western Europe was building nations, southeastern Europe was doing something else: **separating from an empire**.

**In 1821 a Greek uprising broke out.** After nearly a decade of war and great-power intervention, **Greece became independent in 1830** — the first European nation-state to succeed in leaving the Ottoman Empire, and a sign to Christians across the Balkans that it was possible. Serbia won autonomy around the same period and gradually extended it.

The Ottoman retreat soon drew in the great powers. In the **Crimean War of 1853–1856** Britain and France went to war to prevent Russia from swallowing the Ottoman inheritance alone, and the Treaty of Paris followed — the most typical scene of the "Eastern Question": **the subject was Ottoman territory, but the speakers were others.** In 1875–1878 uprisings swept the Balkans again, Russia defeated the Ottomans, and the **Congress of Berlin in 1878** redrew the map: Serbia, Montenegro and Romania became independent, Bulgaria an autonomous principality, and Austria-Hungary acquired the right of occupation over Bosnia-Herzegovina.

| Dates     | Event                | Outcome                                                     |
| --------- | -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1821–1830 | Greek War of Independence | The first European nation-state to leave the Ottoman Empire |
| 1853–1856 | Crimean War          | The powers block Russia's unilateral takeover; the Ottomans survive for now |
| 1878      | Congress of Berlin   | Serbia, Montenegro, Romania independent; Bulgaria autonomous; Austria-Hungary occupies Bosnia-Herzegovina |
| 1908      | Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia-Herzegovina | Triggers the Bosnian Crisis; Austro-Russian rivalry sharpens |
| 1912–1913 | Two Balkan Wars      | The Ottomans lose almost all their European territory       |

By 1913 the Ottomans held only a small area around Istanbul in Europe. **And the Balkans were not thereby at peace**: Bulgaria, Serbia, Greece and Romania immediately fought over the spoils and fell out with one another, while Austria-Hungary and Russia collided head-on in the region. **Most of the gunpowder of the First World War was piled up here.**

{% hint style="info" %}
**The "multinational empire" was not a historical leftover but the norm of the day.** In 1830, of the largest polities in Europe by area — Russia, the Ottoman Empire, the Habsburg lands and the British Empire — not one was a nation-state. The nation-state was merely one form in Europe at the time, and the newest one.
{% endhint %}

## 5.6 Imperial rivalry and the eve of war

1871 — 1914

At the end of the nineteenth century the great powers pursued **colonial rivalry** in Africa and Asia until the world was partitioned. Within Europe rival alignments developed, although their treaty obligations and wartime choices differed: the **Triple Alliance** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the **Triple Entente** (Britain, France, Russia). Italy remained neutral in 1914 despite its membership of the Triple Alliance and joined the Entente side in 1915. Arms racing, national grievance and alliance commitments piled up one on another, turning Europe into a powder keg ready to blow.

On 28 June 1914 Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, was assassinated at **Sarajevo**. An apparently accidental event triggered a chain reaction, and within weeks the powers were drawn into war one after another.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Was the First World War an accident or an inevitability?** Both accounts hold half the truth. In the short run, the miscalculations of the July Crisis did resemble an accident; in the long run, the alliance system, the arms race, colonial rivalry and nationalism had already accumulated enormous structural pressure. The more careful formulation: **the structure supplied the powder, the decisions lit the fuse** — and a good part of the powder lay in the Balkans.
{% endhint %}
