# 08 Common Misconceptions

The most common missteps in reading European history come from two things: confusing four senses of Europe — geographical, cultural, political and organizational — and assuming by default that European history means Western European history. This chapter gathers and supplements misconceptions discussed in the preceding chapters.

<details>

<summary>Does the Roman Empire's "fall" mean Europe entered a dark age?</summary>

No. 476 conventionally marks the end of the western imperial office in Italy; it meant neither the instant disappearance of all Roman institutions nor an absence of substantive change; the Eastern (Byzantine) Empire lasted nearly a thousand years more, and Roman law, the Church and urban life did not break off in the West either. More accurately: the Western imperial administrative framework collapsed, and three forces — the Germanic peoples, the old Roman aristocracy and the Church — began to recombine.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Were the Middle Ages a "dark age"?</summary>

The phrase comes mainly from Renaissance humanists, who belittled the preceding centuries to elevate their own. In fact universities, parliaments, banks, mechanical clocks and Gothic architecture were all born in the Middle Ages: it was the era when Europe's basic structures took shape, not a blank. In the same period the cities and scholarship of Byzantium and the Islamic world were flourishing too — they simply do not appear in the usual story of "medieval Europe".

</details>

<details>

<summary>Has Christianity always been the unifying religion of Europe?</summary>

No. The eastern and western churches separated gradually, with 1054 serving as a conventional symbolic milestone, and the Reformation of the sixteenth century split off Protestantism. The reach of Christendom also extended far beyond Europe, once covering North Africa and western Asia: "Christianity = Europe" is itself a later simplification. And even inside "Christian Europe", the Latin Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant maps never shared a single authority.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was Byzantium merely an "Eastern" empire, with nothing to do with Europe?</summary>

No — and this is precisely the illusion created by a Western-centred narrative. Byzantium was centred on Constantinople, used Greek, called its people "Romans", preserved Roman law and developed the Orthodox tradition, lasting nearly a thousand years. It is the political and religious source of southeastern Europe and the Slavic world. The cultural foundations of Greece, Serbia, Bulgaria, Romania and Russia today all descend from this line.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was Ottoman rule an "episode" in European history?</summary>

Not an episode, but one of the main lines. The Ottomans entered the Balkans in the fourteenth century and ruled or controlled southeastern Europe directly and indirectly for four or five hundred years — longer than many Western dynasties. What they left behind is a world of towns where mosques and Orthodox churches stood side by side, a structure of religious communities under the millet system, and patterns of ethnicity and religion that still shape the region. Filing it under "Asian history" erases the memory of half of Europe.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Has "Europe" always been a single whole or entity?</summary>

No. Europa began as a vague geographical direction. "Europe" as a political and cultural identity was defined only gradually, by contrast with the Islamic world and with outside others.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Is the European Union the same thing as Europe?</summary>

No. The EU was founded in 1993 and is one cooperative arrangement among some present-day European states; not every European country is a member, the United Kingdom left in 2020, and member states have not handed over every part of their sovereignty. Confusing geographical Europe, cultural Europe and the EU produces a great deal of misjudgement. **And it should certainly not be treated as the destination of European history.**

</details>

<details>

<summary>Did the Renaissance appear out of nowhere?</summary>

No. It rested on the rise of universities from the twelfth century, the return of classical texts via the Arabic world, and accumulated urban economic wealth. Nor was it a break with the Middle Ages: it reorganized the nourishment the Middle Ages had stored up. One body of Greek manuscripts entered Italy precisely with the westward move of Byzantine scholars around 1453.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was the Reformation only a dispute over belief?</summary>

Not only. It was also political and social conflict: princes used reform to escape the taxation and jurisdiction of Church and emperor, printing amplified the arguments, and the Peasants' War carried it to the bottom of society. Faith, power and interest were tangled together. It also had geographical limits — large parts of eastern Europe still belonged to the Orthodox world and never experienced "the quarrel between Catholic and Protestant".

</details>

<details>

<summary>Did Columbus "discover" a new world?</summary>

It depends whose standpoint you take. For Europeans it was a discovery; for the indigenous peoples of the Americas it was the beginning of conquest, enslavement and disease, which cut their populations drastically. Today "the age of oceanic exploration" or "colonial expansion" is often preferred, to include the side that was written out.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was the Thirty Years' War purely a religious war?</summary>

Not entirely. It began as religious conflict and became increasingly a contest of dynasty and balance of power: Catholic France entered on the Protestant side in order to weaken the Habsburgs. Religion was the fuse; power was the main line. It is also worth noting that the Ottomans were not among the participants in this "European war" — they were busy on another front.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Did Europe enter the modern age after 1453?</summary>

Broadly true for Western Europe, and exactly the opposite for the southeast. The fall of Constantinople is indeed a conventional period marker, but for Greece, Serbia and Bulgaria it marks the **beginning of Ottoman rule** — a period of more than four hundred years. The same year means completely different things in different regions; there is no single "European timetable".

</details>

<details>

<summary>Did the two world wars wipe out all empires?</summary>

Four empires did collapse after 1918 (the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman), but the imperial form did not disappear with them: the Soviet Union integrated a multinational space in another form, the overseas colonial empires were dismantled only after the Second World War, and the borders and populations of central and eastern Europe were rearranged again after 1945. **The retreat of empire was a long process, not a single year.**

</details>

<details>

<summary>Did the First World War start by accident?</summary>

Both accounts hold half the truth. In the short run the miscalculations of the July Crisis in 1914 did look like an accident; in the long run the alliance system, the arms race, colonial rivalry and nationalism had already accumulated structural pressure. The structure supplied the powder, the decisions lit the fuse — and most of that powder was piled in the Balkans.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was post-war European integration purely an ideal of peace?</summary>

Not entirely. Ideals of peace supplied the direction, but economic interest and Cold War pressure were just as important. Putting coal and steel under a common authority both prevented war and rebuilt the economy against the Eastern bloc: several motives layered together. Note also the scope: this arrangement covered only the continental part of Western Europe from the outset; Eastern Europe was then on the other side.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was Eastern Europe "always" different from Western Europe?</summary>

That is a later narrative. In the Middle Ages Poland, Hungary and Bohemia were closely connected with the West; "Eastern Europe" as a category opposed to "Western Europe" was to a large extent constructed in the eighteenth-century Enlightenment and then reinforced during the Cold War. Treating it as an age-old divide obscures the long interweaving of the two.

</details>

<details>

<summary>After 1989, did Europe become peacefully unified?</summary>

No. The upheavals did end the Cold War dividing line, but in the same period the disintegration of Yugoslavia produced the most brutal war in Europe since 1945 (1991–1999, including the Srebrenica massacre); there have since been conflicts in the Caucasus, Crimea and the Donbas, and Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. **The disappearance of a dividing line is not the disappearance of conflict.**

</details>
