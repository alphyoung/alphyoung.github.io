# 04 Stage 4 Century of Monarchs

After the Thirty Years' War Europe rebuilt its order around sovereign states, and "the strong state" became the consensus: absolute monarchs institutionalized administration, taxation and standing armies. At the same time the scientific revolution handed nature over to mathematical law, and the Enlightenment extended reason from nature to society — the summit of the old order was also the eve of its being questioned.

* **1648** Peace of Westphalia
* **1687** Newton's Principia
* **1756** Seven Years' War
* **1789** French Revolution

## 4.1 Absolute monarchy and "reason of state"

1648 — 1750

After the chaos of the Thirty Years' War, "the strong state" became the fashionable answer. Louis XIV of France domesticated the nobility at Versailles and built an **absolute monarchy** administered directly by central officials (he is said to have declared, "I am the state" — though the line may be a later attribution, it captures his conception of rule). He revoked the Edict of Nantes, suppressed Protestantism, and brought religion too under state management.

Imitators followed in quick succession: the Hohenzollerns in Prussia, the Habsburgs in Austria, Peter the Great in Russia — all building standing armies, unified tax systems and bureaucracies. **"Reason of state"**, which took the interest of the state as the highest criterion, gradually replaced religion as the declared cause of war.

The political logic of the period was to gather dispersed feudal rights into the monarch's hands; and the problem it left behind was equally stark — once the monarch's power was no longer checked by the Church, who was to check the monarch? That question would be answered by the next century's Enlightenment thinkers and revolutionaries.

## 4.2 The scientific revolution: from Copernicus to Newton

1543 — 1687

Alongside the concentration of monarchical power came a revolution in how the world was seen. This thread runs across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and finally changed the way human beings understood nature. **Copernicus** proposed heliocentrism in 1543, shaking the Earth-centred picture of the cosmos; **Galileo** observed the moons of Jupiter through a telescope, challenged dogma with experiment, and was tried for it.

**In 1687 Newton published the Principia Mathematica**, bringing heavens and earth into a single mathematical system through the laws of motion and universal gravitation. Thereafter the universe was understood as a machine that could be calculated and predicted.

The revolution's deeper influence went beyond astronomy. It established a new source of authority: **no longer texts and doctrine, but observation, experiment and reasoning**. "Natural law" became a general framework for explaining the world — and the method would soon be applied to society and politics.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Science and religion were not simply opposed.** Copernicus, Kepler and Newton were themselves largely religious men, and much of the impetus of early modern science came from "understanding the design of the Creator". What was actually broken was the Church's monopoly on explaining nature, not faith itself.
{% endhint %}

## 4.3 The Enlightenment

1687 — 1789

If nature follows laws, might society follow laws too? Eighteenth-century thinkers carried the method and the confidence of science over to human affairs. **Locke** wrote of natural rights and limited government, **Montesquieu** of the separation of powers, **Voltaire** attacked clerical power and arbitrary rule, **Rousseau** proposed popular sovereignty. The **Encyclopédie** edited by Diderot gathered the new knowledge into volumes and spread it widely.

The core demand of the Enlightenment was to move the ground of judgement from tradition and authority to public reason and discussion. It asserted universal human rights and the rule of law, and required room for speech and publication.

Yet the Enlightenment was born in an age of expanding empire and slavery. The contradiction runs through it: the same slogans could be used to demand equality and to justify domination.

{% hint style="info" %}
**The Enlightenment was not a single "movement".** It was full of internal argument: Voltaire and Rousseau fought, British empiricism and French rationalism disagreed at every point. "The spirit of Enlightenment" is better seen as several strands in tension than as one settled doctrine.
{% endhint %}

## 4.4 Global rivalry: mercantilism and colonial competition

1650 — 1789

The balance of power among sovereign states quickly extended overseas. In the seventeenth century the Dutch were pre-eminent through shipping and finance; the English then displaced them through a series of navigation acts and wars. By the eighteenth century Britain and France were the protagonists of global competition.

The guiding economic doctrine was **mercantilism**: bullion and a trade surplus as the measure of national strength, colonies as sources of raw material and markets. This zero-sum thinking bound colonial expansion tightly to interstate war. The **Seven Years' War of 1756–1763** is often called a rehearsal for a world war: its theatres stretched from Europe to North America and India, and afterwards Britain held the advantage at sea and in the colonies.

But the costs accumulated too: taxes levied to share the expense of war eventually inflamed resistance in the North American colonies. In 1776 the **United States declared independence** — the political ideas of the Enlightenment used for the first time to build a new nation.
