# 03 Stage 3 Fractured Faith

The two unities of the Middle Ages broke in this stage at once: the shared faith of Latin Christendom, and the economy centred on the Mediterranean. The Renaissance rediscovered antiquity, oceanic voyages carried rivalry across the globe, and the Reformation tore the faith apart; after the Thirty Years' War the Peace of Westphalia established a new order of co-existing sovereign states.

* **1453** Constantinople falls
* **1492** Columbus reaches the Americas
* **1517** Reformation
* **1555** Peace of Augsburg
* **1648** Peace of Westphalia

## 3.1 Renaissance and humanism

_14th — 16th century_

The change had already been brewing in Italy. Wealthy **city-republics** — Florence, Venice, Milan — patronized artists and scholars, the Medici being the most famous of the patrons. Scholars collected and collated Greek and Roman texts afresh, producing **humanism**: a shift of attention from theological speculation toward human dignity, cultivation and public life.

Technology amplified the movement. **Around 1450 Gutenberg improved movable-type printing**, and books could be reproduced in quantity. Ideas no longer depended on manuscript and word of mouth; the speed and reach of their transmission were utterly transformed — which also made the rapid spread of the coming Reformation possible.

Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael, Machiavelli, Erasmus — they inherited antiquity while looking at reality with new eyes. The Renaissance did not cut itself off from the Middle Ages; it reorganized medieval nourishment in a new way.

And while the Renaissance turned European eyes from the next world to this one, from the Mediterranean to the ocean, the **fall of Constantinople** in 1453 both ended the thousand-year Byzantine Empire and announced the close of the Middle Ages — pushing Europe toward a new beginning.

## 3.2 Oceanic voyages and colonial empires

_1415 — 1600_

The Ottomans took **Constantinople** in 1453 and thereafter controlled the trade routes between the eastern Mediterranean and Eurasia for a long time. Western merchants therefore turned increasingly to the sea — though to attribute overseas expansion entirely to "the blocked trade route" is a simplification: Portuguese voyages had begun as early as 1415. Portugal pushed south along the African coast, rounded the Cape of Good Hope and entered the Indian Ocean; Spain ventured westward — and **in 1492 Columbus reached the Americas**.

Over the following century Spain and Portugal were first to build transoceanic colonial empires, with the Dutch, English and French joining later. American silver poured into Europe and overturned prices and trade; between Africa and the Americas the **transatlantic slave trade** was forcibly assembled, with some twelve million people shipped to the Americas and many more dying in capture or passage.

The deeper consequence of the voyages was a shift in economic gravity: the Mediterranean ceased to be Europe's centre, and the Atlantic ports — Lisbon, Seville, Amsterdam, London — rose in turn. Europe's internal balance of power began to tilt northwest.

{% hint style="warning" %}
**On the phrase "the Age of Discovery":** what was discovery for Europeans was the beginning of catastrophe for the indigenous peoples of the Americas — conquest, enslavement and disease cut their populations drastically. More and more textbooks now speak of "the age of oceanic exploration" or "colonial expansion", to hold within the frame the side that was written out.
{% endhint %}

## 3.3 The Reformation: the fracture of a single faith

_1517 — 1555_

**In 1517 Martin Luther issued the Ninety-five Theses**, questioning indulgences and church authority and asserting justification by faith alone. The Bible was translated into German, and ordinary people could read it for themselves. Calvin built another reforming tradition in Geneva, while Henry VIII of England broke away over his marriage annulment and founded the Church of England. The religious unity Western Europe had kept for a thousand years was broken.

The **Peace of Augsburg of 1555** established _cuius regio, eius religio_ — whose realm, his religion. But this brought no freedom of individual conscience and left Calvinism out; grievances kept accumulating, and half a century later they erupted in a far larger war.

{% hint style="info" %}
**The Reformation was not only a dispute over belief.** It was also a political and social conflict: princes used reform to escape the taxation and jurisdiction of Church and emperor, printing amplified the argument, and the German Peasants' War of 1524–1525 carried it to the bottom of society. Faith, power and interest were tangled together.
{% endhint %}

## 3.4 The Thirty Years' War and the Westphalian system

_1618 — 1648_

The **Thirty Years' War of 1618–1648** was the general eruption of religious and dynastic conflict: beginning with a rebellion in Bohemia, it drew in Spain, Denmark, Sweden and France by stages, reduced much of central Europe to ruins, and cost the German lands especially dearly in population.

The **Peace of Westphalia** of 1648 ended the war. It is usually taken as the starting point of modern international relations: order maintained among sovereign states through treaties and the balance of power, with "sovereignty" now given an institutional footing.

| Principle established        | Meaning                                                              | Later influence                                           |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sovereign equality           | States are mutually independent and owe no allegiance to one another | The germ of the modern idea of the sovereign state        |
| Confession follows territory | Catholicism, Lutheranism and Calvinism recognized as co-existing     | Religious pluralism institutionalized in central Europe   |
| Conflict managed by treaty   | Negotiation and balance of power instead of religious war            | A precedent for diplomacy and the balance-of-power system |

This stage can therefore be summarized thus: **the unity of faith broke apart, and co-existing sovereign states took its place.** Europe would never again have a single shared religious authority, and could only seek equilibrium among many states.
