# 05 Stage 5 Revolution and Nation States

The French Revolution pushed "sovereignty of the people" into the centre of politics, and Napoleon's wars carried the idea across Europe; the Vienna system tried to restore order but could not hold back nationalism and industrialization. By 1914 Europe was a cluster of nation-states armed to the teeth.

* **1789** French Revolution
* **1815** Congress of Vienna
* **1848** Year of Revolutions
* **1861** Italy unified
* **1871** Germany unified
* **1914** First World War

## 5.1 The French Revolution and Napoleon

1789 — 1815

Financial crisis forced Louis XVI to convene the Estates-General, and the gates swung open unexpectedly. **In 1789** the Third Estate formed itself into a National Assembly, the Bastille was stormed, and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen proclaimed popular sovereignty and equality before the law. The Revolution then grew steadily more radical: the king executed, a republic founded, the **Terror** imposed, and then a return to conservatism through the Thermidorian reaction.

In 1799 **Napoleon Bonaparte** took power, and in 1804 had himself crowned emperor. With the Napoleonic Code he institutionalized part of the Revolution's achievement — equality before the law, private property, freedom of contract — and carried it across Europe with his armies. But his conquests also provoked national resistance in the occupied lands, and ended in total defeat at **Waterloo** in 1815.

## 5.2 The Vienna system and nationalism

1815 — 1848

In 1814–1815 the victors redrew Europe at the **Congress of Vienna**. Its manager **Metternich** pursued "equilibrium" and "legitimacy": restoring the dynasties Napoleon had overturned, suppressing revolution and national claims, and maintaining order through a Holy Alliance of Russia, Prussia and Austria. The system kept the peace in outline for nearly forty years.

But two forces were accumulating. The first was **nationalism**: language, history and shared memory were invoked to demand "one people, one state". The second was **liberalism**: demands for constitutions, parliaments and civil rights. In 1848 revolution swept Paris, Vienna, Berlin and Milan — the **Year of Revolutions**. Most of the revolutions were put down, yet they nailed the questions of nationhood and constitutional government firmly onto Europe's agenda.

## 5.3 The Industrial Revolution and social change

1760 — 1900

In the later eighteenth century Britain was first to combine coal, iron and the steam engine: mechanized textiles, laid railways, built the factory system. Industrialization spread from Britain to Belgium, France and Germany, turning Europe into "the workshop of the world". Cities swelled enormously, and population structure, family forms and the sense of time were all reshaped.

The costs were enormous as well: workers laboured long hours in grim conditions, child labour was widespread, and inequality widened. In response came trade unions, cooperatives and socialist thought. The **Communist Manifesto of 1848**, the later First International and the national social-democratic parties were all political products of that contradiction. Industrialization also vastly increased the military and economic capacity of states, setting the stage for the total wars to come.

## 5.4 National unification: Italy and Germany

1848 — 1871

The clearest cases of nationalism turning from an idea into fact are Italy and Germany. Italy had long been divided into many states, some of them under Austrian control. Through **Cavour**'s diplomacy and **Garibaldi**'s campaigns, the **Kingdom of Italy** was proclaimed in 1861; Rome was recovered in 1870 and became the capital in 1871, completing unification.

Germany advanced along the "Lesser German" line, excluding Austria. **Bismarck**, known for "blood and iron", cleared the ground through three wars — against Denmark, Austria and France. **In 1871 the German Empire was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles**, with Wilhelm I as emperor. A new great power had appeared in the middle of Europe, and the balance of power changed with it.

Prussia → North German Confederation → German Empire (1871)

The manner of unification planted trouble too: the German crown was proclaimed on French soil, leaving a long Franco-German grievance; and the new empire's industry and military strength grew very fast, with no matching international arrangement to accommodate them.

## 5.5 Imperial rivalry and the eve of war

1871 — 1914

At the end of the nineteenth century the great powers pursued **colonial rivalry** in Africa and Asia until the world was partitioned. Within Europe two armed blocs formed: the **Triple Alliance** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the **Triple Entente** (Britain, France, Russia). Arms racing, national grievance and alliance commitments piled up one on another, turning Europe into a powder keg ready to blow.

In June 1914 Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, was assassinated at **Sarajevo**. An apparently accidental event triggered a chain reaction, and within weeks the powers were drawn into war one after another.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Was the First World War an accident or an inevitability?** Both accounts hold half the truth. In the short run, the miscalculations of the July Crisis did resemble an accident; in the long run, the alliance system, the arms race, colonial rivalry and nationalism had already accumulated enormous structural pressure. The more careful formulation: the structure supplied the powder, the decisions lit the fuse.
