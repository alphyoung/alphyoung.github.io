# 09 Appendix Concepts and Sources

The stages, concepts and entities that recur throughout are gathered here as reference tables. Half the value of history lies in knowing the facts; the other half lies in knowing where these concepts come from and where their boundaries are.

## A.1 The seven stages at a glance

| Stage                                        | Span            | Theme in one sentence                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Ⅰ Classical foundations                      | 800 BC — AD 476 | Greece, Rome and Christianity converge in the Mediterranean; the empire forks east and west |
| Ⅱ Middle Ages                                | 476 — 1453      | Latin Europe, Byzantium and the Orthodox world run in parallel; the Ottomans appear |
| Ⅲ Re-drawing the boundaries                  | 1453 — 1648     | Faith fractures and economic gravity shifts west; the southeast enters the Ottoman world |
| Ⅳ Century of monarchs                        | 1648 — 1789     | Monarchs centralize; Russia, Prussia and Austria rise, Poland vanishes, the Ottomans retreat |
| Ⅴ Revolution, nation and empire              | 1789 — 1914     | Nation-states and multinational empires coexist; the Balkans in continuous turmoil |
| Ⅵ Two world wars and the end of empires      | 1914 — 1945     | Four empires collapse; borders and populations of central and eastern Europe redrawn |
| Ⅶ Division and reassembly                    | 1945 — present  | Cold War division, the upheavals of 1989, and Europe pieced together again        |

## A.2 Key concepts

| Concept            | Meaning                                                                 | Common misconception                                                       |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| City-state (polis) | A political unit constituted by a community of citizens                 | Mistaken for a modern state; its citizen body was in fact extremely narrow |
| Roman Republic (specific case) | A regime organizing power through a senate, elections and fixed terms | The assumption that the Roman Republic was democracy for all               |
| Feudalism          | A hierarchy of relationships maintained by land grants and allegiance   | Pictured as a tidy bureaucratic pyramid; also assumed to describe all of Europe |
| Millet system      | The Ottoman arrangement for governing non-Muslim subjects by religious community | Mistaken for religious toleration in the modern sense |
| Sovereignty        | The supreme power of a state within its territory                       | The assumption that it is ancient; it is a modern product                  |
| Nation-state       | A state whose legitimacy rests on national identity                     | Believing that "one people, one state" is a natural condition              |
| National self-determination | The right of a people to found its own state                   | Assuming it applies naturally to every group inside an empire              |
| Balance of power   | Preventing any one power from dominating, and so preserving order       | Assuming that balance necessarily brings peace                             |
| The Eastern Question | The long contest among the great powers over the Ottoman inheritance  | Mistaking it for an internal Ottoman affair                                |
| Integration        | States exercising part of their sovereignty through common institutions | Assuming it means creating a single "European state", or a direction for all of Europe |

## A.3 Major political entities in sequence

| Polity                          | Approximate dates      | Key thread                                                    |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Greek city-states               | From c. 800 BC; 146 BC marks a Roman conquest milestone           | The source of citizen politics, philosophy and drama          |
| Roman Republic / Empire         | 509 BC — AD 476 (West) | Law, provinces and Mediterranean unity                        |
| Byzantine Empire                | c. 330 / 395 — 1453 (conventional periodization)             | Orthodoxy, Greek, a thousand years of continuity              |
| Kievan Rus → Russia             | From the 9th century; empire from 1721 | Orthodoxy, the Slavic world, later a Eurasian empire          |
| Carolingian Empire              | 800 — partition in 843 (not the end of Carolingian rule)    | The first large-scale integration of the West                 |
| Holy Roman Empire               | 962 — 1806             | A composite framework of diverse territories and polities, not one nation-state  |
| Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth  | 1569 — 1795            | Noble republic and religious toleration, finally partitioned three times |
| Ottoman Empire (European lands) | 14th century — 1922    | Long rule over the Balkans and southeastern Europe            |
| Austria-Hungary                 | 1867 — 1918            | A dual multinational empire, dissolved in the First World War |
| European Union                  | 1993 — present         | A regional arrangement among some European states based on pooled sovereignty |

## A.4 Suggestions for going further

* **Take the trunk first:** build a framework with the seven stages and the overall timeline, then fill in detail — more effective than memorizing events in isolation.
* **Then separate the threads:** religion, economics, war and ideas can each be followed on their own, to see how they pull on one another.
* **Add the other half of the map:** after reading the Western European story, follow the Byzantine–Orthodox–Ottoman line through again; many "European commonplaces" turn out to be Western European commonplaces.
* **Watch the perspective:** the same event is told very differently from different positions. Of any "common-sense" conclusion it is worth asking: whose account is this?
