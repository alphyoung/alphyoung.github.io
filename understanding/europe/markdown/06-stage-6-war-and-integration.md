# 06 Stage 6 War and Integration

Two world wars pushed Europe from the centre of the world into ruin, and the Cold War cut the continent in two. It was precisely under the lesson of war and the pressure of the Cold War that Europe took an unprecedented path — trading part of its sovereignty for peace and prosperity. After 1989–1991 the continent was pieced back together, and new tensions came with it.

* **1914** World War I begins
* **1919** Treaty of Versailles
* **1939** World War II begins
* **1945** World War II ends
* **1957** Treaty of Rome
* **1989** Berlin Wall falls
* **1991** USSR dissolves

## 6.1 The First World War and the Versailles system

**1914 — 1929**

The **First World War** of 1914–1918 was Europe's first industrialized total war: trenches, machine guns, gas and artillery consumed an entire generation. Four empires — German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman — collapsed or disintegrated in the course of it.

The **Treaty of Versailles** of 1919 imposed territorial cessions, armament limits and heavy reparations on a defeated Germany, and created the **League of Nations**. But the settlement placed war guilt on Germany alone and failed to build effective machinery for enforcement or mediation. The Weimar Republic struggled through inflation and humiliation, and the **Great Depression** of 1929 made matters worse.

## 6.2 Totalitarianism and the Second World War

**1929 — 1945**

The Great Depression destroyed confidence in liberal democracy. Italian **fascism**, German **Nazism** and the Soviet **Stalinist system** all offered some form of total mobilization as the answer. Hitler came to power in 1933 and tore up the Versailles settlement step by step, annexing Austria and Czechoslovakia. **In 1939 Germany invaded Poland**, and the Second World War began in full.

This was a genuinely global war, and the scene of the most terrible atrocities in human history: its theatres covered Europe, North Africa and Asia-Pacific, and the **Holocaust** systematically murdered six million Jews along with vast numbers of other groups. Germany surrendered unconditionally in 1945, and Europe set out again from amid ruins and moral shock.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Where did post-war European integration come from?** One immediate motive was to prevent such a war from ever happening again. France and Germany, the two hereditary enemies, chose to place coal and steel — the most basic raw materials of war — under a common authority, replacing military confrontation with economic entanglement.
{% endhint %}

## 6.3 Cold War: a Europe divided

**1945 — 1989**

Wartime alliance quickly turned into confrontation. The Western bloc led by the United States rebuilt Western Europe's economy through the **Marshall Plan** (from 1948) and founded **NATO** in 1949; the Soviet Union built a sphere of influence in Eastern Europe and organized the **Warsaw Pact** in 1955. The "Iron Curtain" ran from the Baltic to the Adriatic, and Europe was cut in two.

The symbol of that division was the **Berlin Wall** (built in 1961). One city was split by a wall and a mine belt, each side running a different system. Eastern Europe under Soviet influence adopted planned economies and one-party rule; Hungary in 1956 and the "Prague Spring" of 1968 were reform attempts that were suppressed.

## 6.4 European integration: from the Coal and Steel Community to the EU

**1951 — 2004**

In 1951 France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg signed the Treaty of Paris, and the following year formally established the **European Coal and Steel Community**, placing the resources of war under common management. In 1957 the six signed the **Treaty of Rome**, and in 1958 the European Economic Community came into being, moving step by step toward a customs union and a common market. Integration thereafter widened and deepened: institutions, a single market, free movement of people.

In 1993 the **Treaty on European Union (Maastricht)** entered into force and the **European Union** was formally established; from 1999 the euro was used as a unit of account, and from 2002 it circulated. In 2004 the EU undertook its largest **eastern enlargement**, admitting ten countries at once (the eight in central and eastern Europe plus Malta and Cyprus), stitching up Europe's dividing line institutionally.

## 6.5 After 1989: Europe reassembled

**1989 — Present**

In 1989 the **Berlin Wall fell**; Germany reunified in 1990; in **1991 the Soviet Union dissolved**, and the Cold War ended peacefully. The countries of central and eastern Europe turned toward multi-party politics and market economies, and applied in succession to join the EU and NATO. At the same time Yugoslavia's disintegration produced brutal warfare — a reminder that the national question did not disappear along with the Cold War.

Europe today moves forward between achievement and tension: integration has brought lasting peace and prosperity, and has also sharpened arguments over sovereignty, welfare, migration and identity. To understand those arguments one still has to return to the six stages above — Europe has never been a frozen entity, but an object continually reassembled.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Is integration an ideal or an interest?** Both. Political ideals supplied the direction; economic interests and Cold War pressure supplied the motive force. Precisely because it depends on both, integration swings and stalls whenever interests diverge or external pressure changes.
{% endhint %}
