# 07 Common Misconceptions

The most common missteps in reading European history come from confusing four senses of Europe — geographical, cultural, political and organizational. Each of the points below was developed in the corresponding chapter; here they are brought together for checking.

<details>

<summary>Does the Roman Empire's "fall" mean Europe entered a dark age?</summary>

No. What ended in 476 was only the title of the Western emperor; the Eastern (Byzantine) Empire lasted nearly a thousand years more, and Roman law, the Church and urban life did not break off in the West either. More accurately: the Western imperial administrative framework collapsed, and three forces — the Germanic peoples, the old Roman aristocracy and the Church — began to recombine.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Were the Middle Ages a "dark age"?</summary>

The phrase comes mainly from Renaissance humanists, who belittled the preceding centuries to elevate their own. In fact universities, parliaments, banks, mechanical clocks and Gothic architecture were all born in the Middle Ages: it was the era when Europe's basic structures took shape, not a blank.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Has Christianity always been the unifying religion of Europe?</summary>

No. After the East–West Schism of 1054, Catholicism and Orthodoxy went separate ways, and the Reformation of the sixteenth century split off Protestantism. The reach of Christendom also extended far beyond Europe, once covering North Africa and western Asia: "Christianity = Europe" is itself a later simplification.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Has "Europe" always been a single whole or entity?</summary>

No. Europa began as a vague geographical direction. "Europe" as a political and cultural identity was defined only gradually, by contrast with the Islamic world and with outside others.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Is the European Union the same thing as Europe?</summary>

No. The EU was founded in 1993 and is a specific contemporary organization; not every European country is a member, and not everything in a member state is covered by the union. Confusing geographical Europe, cultural Europe and the EU produces a great deal of misjudgement.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Did the Renaissance appear out of nowhere?</summary>

No. It rested on the rise of universities from the twelfth century, the return of classical texts via the Arabic world, and accumulated urban economic wealth. Nor was it a break with the Middle Ages: it reorganized the nourishment the Middle Ages had stored up.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was the Reformation only a dispute over belief?</summary>

Not only. It was also political and social conflict: princes used reform to escape the taxation and jurisdiction of Church and emperor, printing amplified the arguments, and the Peasants' War carried it to the bottom of society. Faith, power and interest were tangled together.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Did Columbus "discover" a new world?</summary>

It depends whose standpoint you take. For Europeans it was a discovery; for the indigenous peoples of the Americas it was the beginning of conquest, enslavement and disease, which cut their populations drastically. Today "the age of oceanic exploration" or "colonial expansion" is often preferred, to include the side that was written out.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was the Thirty Years' War purely a religious war?</summary>

Not entirely. It began as religious conflict and became increasingly a contest of dynasty and balance of power: Catholic France entered on the Protestant side in order to weaken the Habsburgs. Religion was the fuse; power was the main line.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Did the First World War start by accident?</summary>

Both accounts hold half the truth. In the short run the miscalculations of the July Crisis in 1914 did look like an accident; in the long run the alliance system, the arms race, colonial rivalry and nationalism had already accumulated structural pressure. The structure supplied the powder, the decisions lit the fuse.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was post-war European integration purely an ideal of peace?</summary>

Not entirely. Ideals of peace supplied the direction, but economic interest and Cold War pressure were just as important. Putting coal and steel under a common authority both prevented war and rebuilt the economy against the Eastern bloc: several motives layered together.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Was Eastern Europe "always" different from Western Europe?</summary>

That is a later narrative. In the Middle Ages Poland, Hungary and Bohemia were closely connected with the West; "Eastern Europe" as a category opposed to "Western Europe" was to a large extent constructed in the eighteenth-century Enlightenment and then reinforced during the Cold War. Treating it as an age-old divide obscures the long interweaving of the two.

</details>
