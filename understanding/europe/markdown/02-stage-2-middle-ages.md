# 02 Stage 2 Middle Ages

The Middle Ages were not a "dark blank" but the era in which Europe's basic structures took shape: feudal contract, the manorial economy, church authority, universities and towns all grew in this period. Charlemagne's coronation in 800 opened the first integration of the West; the schism of 1054 divided the churches; the plague and wars of the fourteenth century drove this order into deep crisis.

## 2.1 The Franks and Charlemagne: the first integration of Western Europe

476 — 843

After the Western Empire dissolved, Germanic peoples founded a series of kingdoms on its former soil: the Visigoths in Iberia, the Ostrogoths in Italy, the Franks in Gaul. Of these the Frankish kingdom had the greatest potential. Clovis of the Merovingian dynasty converted to Catholic Christianity, linking royal power to the Church and to the Romanized Gallic aristocracy.

By the eighth century the Carolingians had supplanted them. **In 800 Charlemagne was crowned "Emperor of the Romans" in Rome by the pope** — a deeply significant gesture: it both continued the Roman imagination and made imperial power dependent upon papal coronation for recognition. This was the first large-scale integration of the West since the fall of Rome.

But integration was fragile. In 843 the **Treaty of Verdun** divided Charlemagne's empire into three, roughly prefiguring the later France, Germany and Italy — though this was only one of several possible outcomes, not a foregone conclusion.

## 2.2 Feudalism, manors and knights

9th — 13th century

Where central power was weak, order was maintained by personal contracts layer upon layer. This is what is usually called **feudalism**: a lord granted land (a fief) to a vassal, who repaid it with military service and loyalty, and who might in turn enfeoff vassals of his own. It produced a loose hierarchy of relationships, not a state in the modern sense.

The economic unit on the land was the **manor**: the lord held land and jurisdiction, while serfs or peasants farmed and paid rent and labour dues. The overwhelming majority of the population lived in the countryside, at the mercy of the weather. Alongside them stood the **knights** — exchanging armed service for land, and gradually forming a code of honour and loyalty.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Do not picture "feudal" as a tidy pyramid.** Several claims of right might exist over the same plot, and it was normal for a vassal to owe loyalty to more than one lord. Medieval politics was a collage held together by customary law and negotiation, not a clear bureaucratic hierarchy.
{% endhint %}

## 2.3 Church and emperor: the long tug of war

1075 — 1250

The most important thread in medieval Europe was the relationship between the **Church** and **secular rulers**. The Church had its own law, courts and taxation, and vast landed estates: it was not merely a community of believers but a transnational institution of power.

The **Humiliation of Canossa** in 1077 is the famous scene of this contest: Holy Roman Emperor Henry IV, worsted in the Investiture Controversy with Pope Gregory VII, was forced to do penance in the snow. In 1122 the **Concordat of Worms** struck a compromise, each side conceding ground on the appointment of bishops.

The Church also reformed itself from within: the monastic movement and the mendicant orders (Franciscans, Dominicans) both purified devotion and extended influence. By the thirteenth century papal authority reached its peak — and also laid the ground for a long conflict with national monarchs.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Another turning point:** in 1054 Rome and Constantinople excommunicated each other — the **East–West Schism**. Western Catholicism and the Orthodoxy of eastern and southeastern Europe thereafter went separate ways, splitting Europe's religious map in two. That fissure is visible still.
{% endhint %}

## 2.4 Crusades, commercial revival and towns

1096 — 1300

In 1095 Pope Urban II called at Clermont for the recovery of the Holy Land, and the **First Crusade (from 1096)** set out. Over the following two centuries the West launched several campaigns with limited results but far-reaching consequences: they sharpened confrontation with the Islamic and Orthodox worlds, and brought Eastern goods and knowledge back to the West.

Mediterranean trade flourished at the same time. The Italian cities — **Venice, Genoa, Pisa** — accumulated wealth through re-export trade, while the Baltic–North Sea region produced the **Hanseatic League**. Urban self-government, guilds and banks appeared, and an urban economy distinct from the manor took shape.

Cities needed literate men. From the twelfth century, **universities** appeared at Bologna, Paris and Oxford. Classical texts re-entered Western Europe through translations from the Arabic world, quietly accumulating the conditions for the Renaissance to come.

## 2.5 The Black Death and the crisis of the Middle Ages

1347 — 1453

**In 1347 the Black Death came ashore in Europe via the Black Sea and Sicily**, and over the following years carried off roughly a third of the population, perhaps more. Labour collapsed, the manorial economy was shaken, social tensions sharpened, and religious authority was questioned. The shock this plague gave Europe's structures went far beyond that of a mere disease.

Alongside the plague came war and division: the **Hundred Years' War (1337–1453)** ran for more than a century and awakened national consciousness in both England and France; the Church suffered a **Great Schism** (1378–1417), at one point with two or even three popes at once. The medieval illusion of unity was dissolving.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Where does "the Dark Ages" come from?** Chiefly from Renaissance humanists: to throw their own recovery of antiquity into relief, they deliberately dismissed the intervening centuries as "dark". Historians today are more inclined to see the Middle Ages as a creative era — universities, parliaments, banks and mechanical clocks were all born in it.
{% endhint %}
