# 02 Stage 2 Middle Ages

The Middle Ages were not a "dark blank" but the era in which Europe's basic structures took shape: feudal contract, the manorial economy, church authority, universities and towns all grew in this period. But "medieval Europe" never had only one centre: while Franks and popes crowned each other in the West, Byzantium still called itself Rome, the Slavic peoples were entering the Christian world from the Dnieper and the Baltic, and at the far end of Anatolia a new power was rising.

## 2.1 The Franks and Charlemagne: the first integration of Western Europe

476 — 843

After the Western Empire dissolved, Germanic peoples founded a series of kingdoms on its former soil: the Visigoths in Iberia, the Ostrogoths in Italy, the Franks in Gaul. Of these the Frankish kingdom had the greatest potential. Clovis of the Merovingian dynasty converted to Catholic Christianity, linking royal power to the Church and to the Romanized Gallic aristocracy.

By the eighth century the Carolingians had supplanted them. **In 800 Charlemagne was crowned "Emperor of the Romans" in Rome by the pope** — a deeply significant gesture: it both continued the Roman imagination and made imperial power dependent upon papal coronation for recognition. This was the first large-scale integration of the West since the fall of Rome.

But integration was fragile. In 843 the **Treaty of Verdun** divided Charlemagne's empire into three, roughly prefiguring the later France, Germany and Italy — though this was only one of several possible outcomes, not a foregone conclusion.

**Note the ambition and the awkwardness of the title "Emperor of the Romans."** When Charlemagne was crowned, Constantinople still had a Roman emperor in the literal sense. Thereafter "Rome" became a name that could be granted and contested in the West — in 962 Otto I was crowned and the Holy Roman Empire took shape, an entity that was neither Roman, nor reliably holy, nor much of an empire.
{% endhint %}

## 2.2 Feudalism, manors and knights

9th — 13th century

Where central power was weak, order was maintained by personal contracts layer upon layer. This is what is usually called **feudalism**: a lord granted land (a fief) to a vassal, who repaid it with military service and loyalty, and who might in turn enfeoff vassals of his own. It produced a loose hierarchy of relationships, not a state in the modern sense.

The economic unit on the land was the **manor**: the lord held land and jurisdiction, while serfs or peasants farmed and paid rent and labour dues. The overwhelming majority of the population lived in the countryside, at the mercy of the weather. Alongside them stood the **knights** — exchanging armed service for land, and gradually forming a code of honour and loyalty.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Do not picture "feudal" as a tidy pyramid.** Several claims of right might exist over the same plot, and it was normal for a vassal to owe loyalty to more than one lord. Medieval politics was a collage held together by customary law and negotiation, not a clear bureaucratic hierarchy. This arrangement describes Latin Western Europe reasonably well; Byzantium and eastern Europe took a different road, where imperial authority, bureaucracy and the village community mattered more.
{% endhint %}

## 2.3 Church and emperor: the long tug of war

1075 — 1250

The most important thread in medieval Europe was the relationship between the **Church** and **secular rulers**. The Church had its own law, courts and taxation, and vast landed estates: it was not merely a community of believers but a transnational institution of power.

The **Humiliation of Canossa** in 1077 is the famous scene of this contest: Henry IV, king of the Germans (crowned emperor only in 1084), worsted in the Investiture Controversy with Pope Gregory VII, was forced to do penance in the snow. In 1122 the **Concordat of Worms** struck a compromise, each side conceding ground on the appointment of bishops.

The Church also reformed itself from within: the monastic movement and the mendicant orders (Franciscans, Dominicans) both purified devotion and extended influence. By the thirteenth century papal authority reached its peak — and also laid the ground for a long conflict with national monarchs.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Another turning point:** in 1054 Roman legates and the patriarchal authorities in Constantinople issued censures against particular persons. This became a symbolic milestone in the **East–West Schism**, a prolonged separation rather than an instantaneous division of Europe’s entire religious map. Differences of language, rite and political allegiance had been accumulating for centuries before.
{% endhint %}

## 2.4 Crusades, commercial revival and towns

1096 — 1300

In 1095 Pope Urban II called at Clermont for the recovery of the Holy Land, and the **First Crusade (from 1096)** set out. Over the following two centuries the West launched several campaigns with limited results but far-reaching consequences: they sharpened confrontation with the Islamic and Orthodox worlds, and brought Eastern goods and knowledge back to the West.

**The heaviest blow to eastern Europe came in 1204.** The Fourth Crusade, originally aimed at Egypt, ended by storming and plundering **Constantinople** — a city of the same Christian world. The imperial capital, unbreached by an outside enemy since 330, was devastated, the Byzantine Empire was briefly dismembered, and only in 1261 did the empire of Nicaea recover the city. This stab in the back was one of the decisive breaches that made the schism between the churches impossible to mend.

Mediterranean trade flourished at the same time. The Italian cities — **Venice, Genoa, Pisa** — accumulated wealth through re-export trade, while the Baltic–North Sea region produced the **Hanseatic League**. Urban self-government, guilds and banks appeared, and an urban economy distinct from the manor took shape.

Cities needed literate men. During the eleventh and twelfth centuries, **universities** gradually took shape at Bologna, Paris and Oxford; Bologna conventionally dates its foundation to 1088, while teaching at Oxford is recorded in 1096. Classical texts re-entered Western Europe through translations from the Arabic world, quietly accumulating the conditions for the Renaissance to come.

## 2.5 Eastern Europe: Byzantium, the Slavs and the Orthodox world

6th — 14th century

While medieval Western Europe tugged back and forth between feudal contract, church and crown, the east of Europe ran on a different logic.

**Byzantium carried on the Roman state machine:** the emperor was both secular ruler and the leading figure in church affairs; law, taxation and a standing bureaucracy were all in place, and urban life was far more flourishing than in the contemporary West. It had its own expansionary phase — Justinian recovered Italy and North Africa in the sixth century — but thereafter faced sustained pressure from Persia, the Arabs and the Turks, and its territory gradually contracted to Anatolia and the Balkans.

**The Slavic peoples entered history along two routes.** One set out from the Dnieper: **Kievan Rus**, centred on Kiev, took shape in the ninth and tenth centuries (862 is the conventional starting date), and **in 988 Vladimir I was baptized**, making Orthodoxy the religion of Rus. The other ran along the southern Baltic and through central Europe: **Mieszko I of Poland was baptized around 966**, **Hungary was crowned and converted under Stephen around 1000**, while Bohemia long remained within the orbit of the German and Latin Church. **Sharing one Christian faith, yet belonging to two worlds — Rome and Constantinople.** That line has never disappeared in the thousand years since.

{% hint style="info" %}
**The different fates of three "frontier kingdoms."** Poland, Hungary and Bohemia all chose Latin Christianity. That made them culturally part of the West, while politically they long sat in the gap between the Holy Roman Empire and the Teutonic Order, the Mongol invasions, and later Ottoman expansion. What we now call "central and eastern Europe" derives its distinctiveness precisely from this position: part of Western Europe, and continuously struck by forces from the east and the south.
{% endhint %}

## 2.6 The Ottomans appear

13th — 15th century

Just as Byzantium grew weaker, a new protagonist emerged in northwestern Anatolia. The **Ottomans** were originally a Turkic people following the Seljuks, who founded a state in Asia Minor at the end of the thirteenth century and pushed rapidly into the Balkans during the fourteenth.

**The capture of Adrianople (Edirne) in 1361** moved their political centre onto the European side; **the Battle of Kosovo in 1389** broke Serbian power, and Serbia, Bulgaria and Wallachia submitted one after another. In 1396 a crusade of Western knights was crushed at **Nicopolis**. By the middle of the fifteenth century Byzantium was reduced to Constantinople itself and a few holdings in the Peloponnese.

On this page of European history, then, **the Ottomans were not an outside "Asiatic force" but a European polity**: they collected taxes and recruits in the Balkans (including the famous janissary system), enfeoffed vassals, and ruled for four or five hundred years, profoundly changing the languages, religions and populations of southeastern Europe.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Why is this so often overlooked?** Because European historiography since the nineteenth century has taken Latin Western Europe as its main line and placed the southeast in the position of an "imperial frontier". But for the descendants of a Serbian peasant or a Greek merchant, the history after 1389 or 1453 is the history of living with the Ottomans — and that is European history, just not Western European history.
{% endhint %}

## 2.7 The Black Death and the crisis of the Middle Ages

1347 — 1453

**In 1347 the Black Death came ashore in Europe via the Black Sea and Sicily**, and over the following years carried off roughly a third of the population, perhaps more. Labour collapsed, the manorial economy was shaken, social tensions sharpened, and religious authority was questioned. The shock this plague gave Europe's structures went far beyond that of a mere disease.

Alongside the plague came war and division: the **Hundred Years' War (1337–1453)** ran for more than a century and awakened national consciousness in both England and France; the Church suffered a **Great Schism** (1378–1417), at one point with two or even three popes at once. In the east, the last remaining Byzantine territory was being shaved away piece by piece by the Ottomans.

{% hint style="info" %}
**Where does "the Dark Ages" come from?** Chiefly from Renaissance humanists: to throw their own recovery of antiquity into relief, they deliberately dismissed the intervening centuries as "dark". Historians today are more inclined to see the Middle Ages as a creative era — universities, parliaments, banks and mechanical clocks were all born in it. In the eastern half, too, this was an age of flourishing cities, monasteries and trade.
{% endhint %}
