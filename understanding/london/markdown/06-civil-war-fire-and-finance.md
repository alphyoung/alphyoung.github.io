# Chapter Six — Civil War, Fire and Finance: Politics, buildings and finance reshaped London

*1642 – 1694*

> A king executed, a city burned, and new institutions for public finance.

## Politics, buildings and finance reshaped London

### 1. The king was brought to trial

In 1642 Charles I entered the House of Commons with guards to arrest five members. The attempt failed; he left London and civil war broke out. London’s money, militia and printing networks became pillars of the parliamentary cause, though there were royalists in the city too, and Parliament’s own camp and the Puritans were divided among themselves.

In 1649 Charles I was tried in Westminster Hall, then walked out through the Banqueting House at Whitehall onto a scaffold erected in the street and was executed. In 1660 Charles II returned to London and the monarchy was restored. The Act of Uniformity of 1662 then forced a large number of ministers who would not accept the national church’s liturgy out of the Church of England.

### 2. The Great Fire of 1666

After the great plague of 1665, the fire of September 1666 burned a large part of the City of London’s housing, churches and commercial space. Although grid-plan schemes for a new city appeared afterwards, property rights and the urgency of getting life going again meant that many streets were rebuilt on their old lines; brick and fire-prevention rules changed the buildings instead.

> **A small story · Pepys buries his cheese in the garden**
>
> The official Samuel Pepys recorded the fire in his diary. As the flames approached his house he moved his papers and valuables, and in the garden dug a hole to bury his wine and a precious Parmesan cheese. A vast urban disaster keeps the texture of ordinary life because of that one cheese.

Christopher Wren directed the rebuilding of St Paul’s Cathedral and helped design many of the city churches. The winding medieval lanes of the City existing alongside post-fire churches is precisely the result of rebuilding along the old city.

### 3. Constrained monarchy, powerful finance

James II fled in 1688. William III and Mary II accepted the English throne jointly in February 1689. The Bill of Rights of 1689 limited the monarch’s powers; at the same time, war with France required stable financing, and the Bank of England was founded in 1694. Royal power was constrained by Parliament, but the state’s capacity to borrow became stronger.

London’s banks, insurance, stock dealing and port drove one another, and its wealth was closely tied to colonial expansion, the slave trade and plantation production in the Caribbean. The surviving nineteenth-century warehouses of the West India Docks, opened in 1802, are a later reminder that the prosperity of a financial city cannot be separated from the labour and violence behind the goods.
