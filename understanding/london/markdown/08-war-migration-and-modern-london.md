# Chapter Eight — War, Migration and Modern London: An imperial capital becomes a world city

*1940 – present*

> The Blitz, the Windrush generation, the docks that left, and the making of Greater London.

## An imperial capital becomes a world city

### 1. Air raids entered daily life

During the Blitz of 1940–1941, German aircraft repeatedly bombed London, damaging housing, docks and public buildings. Residents took shelter in air-raid shelters and Underground stations, and children were evacuated to the countryside. In the later war years V-1 flying bombs and V-2 rockets brought new attacks.

> **A small story · The people who guarded St Paul’s**
>
> During the raids, cathedral staff and volunteer fire watchers went up onto the roof of St Paul’s Cathedral and put out incendiaries before they could take hold. After the heavy bombing of December 1940, a photograph of the dome standing amid the smoke became a famous wartime image. But the survival of the dome did not mean that the surrounding residents were safe: large districts of the City had been destroyed.

Post-war reconstruction did not only repair old buildings; it built new housing, roads and cultural facilities. The area now occupied by the Barbican was replanned on badly damaged older streets.

### 2. Migration kept changing what “Londoner” meant

London has always been shaped by migration: inhabitants of the Roman empire, medieval European merchants, Huguenot refugees, Irish labourers and Eastern European Jewish immigrants all built communities here. After the Second World War, people from the Caribbean, South Asia and Africa changed transport, healthcare, food, music and everyday language still further.

The arrival of the *Empire Windrush* in Britain in 1948 is an important symbol of post-war Caribbean migration, but it was not the starting point of black presence in London’s history. Many new residents worked in public services while facing discrimination in housing and employment, and organised in their communities to claim equal rights.

### 3. As port activity moved downstream

Container shipping and larger vessels gradually moved port functions downstream along the Thames, and the old docklands went through closures and unemployment in the second half of the twentieth century. Later, office towers rose at Canary Wharf, and finance and services replaced part of the docks’ labour.

New investment brought jobs and transport, but also pushed up land prices and changed communities. Old warehouses, docks, council housing and glass towers stand side by side, recording the different roles the same ground has played in imperial trade, industrial decline and financial globalisation.

### 4. The making of Greater London

In 1965 the new Greater London system came into operation, covering the City of London and thirty-two London boroughs. The Greater London Council was abolished in 1986. A new Greater London Authority, comprising an elected Mayor and London Assembly, began operating in 2000. Today it sits alongside the borough councils and the City of London Corporation. Its boundary is the result of long expansion and administrative reform, not simply an outer ring where the Roman city naturally grew.
