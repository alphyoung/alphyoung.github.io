# Chapter Four — Renaissance London: Print, humanism and the theatre

*1476 – 1600s*

> New ideas arrived on the page and ended up on the stage.

## How new ideas became city life

### 1. The Renaissance entered London through the page

In the later fifteenth century classical learning, humanism and printing reached England in turn. William Caxton set up his press at Westminster in 1476, and English books began to circulate faster and more cheaply. Booksellers, schools and preachers gathered around St Paul’s Cathedral, making London a centre of intellectual exchange.

Printing also made religious controversy harder to control. Bible translations, sermons and political pamphlets could be reproduced quickly, and Crown and church responded with licensing, censorship and punishment. The Renaissance and the Reformation were not two unrelated threads: both depended on the spread of text.

### 2. The contradictory life of Thomas More

The humanist Thomas More served as a sheriff of the City of London and later as Lord Chancellor to Henry VIII. In *Utopia* he imagined a different social order, yet he also took part in persecuting religious dissenters. Refusing to acknowledge the king’s supreme religious authority, he was executed in 1535.

> **A small story · One name, two memories**
>
> More is remembered both as a scholar and a man of conscience, and criticised for suppressing dissent. His life is a reminder that the Renaissance did not automatically mean tolerance, and that men steeped in classical learning also lived inside a harsh religious politics.

### 3. Court culture and urban consumption

The Tudor court brought painting, music, architecture and etiquette from Europe. Artists such as Hans Holbein painted the royal family and the London elite, and dress, heraldry and interior furnishing became instruments for displaying status. At the same time wealthier merchants bought books, maps, musical instruments and imported luxuries, so cultural taste was no longer confined to the palace.

### 4. London in the theatre

In the later sixteenth century London’s population grew, and literacy and commercial entertainment grew with it. Public playhouses were mostly built outside the city walls, where the City authorities had less jurisdiction: the Theatre in Shoreditch, and the Rose and the Globe on the south bank of the Thames. Shakespeare’s plays were performed for paying audiences by commercial companies, with spectators ranging from apprentices and merchants to nobles.

> **Reading the site · Why the Globe stood on the south bank**
>
> The City authorities worried that playhouses brought crowding, disease and disorder. The south bank also gathered taverns, bear-baiting rings and other entertainments. The siting of theatres was therefore also a product of administrative boundaries: cross one bridge and the city’s rules could be different.
