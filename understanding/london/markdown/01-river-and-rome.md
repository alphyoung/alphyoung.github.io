# Chapter One — River and Rome: Where London begins

*AD 43 – 5th century*

> A river crossing, a Roman foundation, and the first time the settlement was destroyed.

## The Thames and Londinium

London had a river before it had a city. The Thames flows east towards the sea and connects London with the interior to the west — good for shipping, and demanding reliable fords and bridges. After the Roman invasion of Britain began in AD 43, **Londinium** was founded on the north bank around AD 47–50. Bridges, wharves and roads quickly made it a commercial and administrative centre.

### 1. “Celtic” was not a single people

Before the Roman conquest, Britain was made up of many local communities. Today they are often lumped together as “the Celts”, but the Catuvellauni, the Trinovantes, the Iceni and others each had their own leaders and territories. The Iron Age inhabitants around London never built a town that corresponds directly to Roman Londinium.

> **A small story · Boudica burned the young London**
>
> In AD 60 or 61 Boudica, queen of the Iceni, rose against Rome. Judging his forces too few, the Roman governor gave up the defence of Londinium; many residents who could not get away in time were killed and the town was burned. Burn layers in the archaeological record remain the evidence of that war to this day. Boudica came from East Anglia — she was not a “queen of London”.

### 2. An imperial city

Rebuilt Londinium had public baths, temples, a forum and a busy port. A large riverside building complex has been interpreted as the governor’s palace, but its identification remains debated. Its people came from across the empire — merchants, soldiers and craftsmen among them, and enslaved people as well. The landward wall, built around AD 200, would shape the boundary of the City of London for centuries afterwards.

After Roman rule ended in the early fifth century, urban life that depended on imperial administration and trade declined markedly. Seeing Roman remains beneath Tube stations, office blocks and streets today does not mean that London has kept the same size and the same centre for two thousand years.

> **Reading the site · Why the Roman city lies underground**
>
> Collapsed buildings, raised road levels and repeated rebuilding lift the ground surface. Looking at the surviving stretches of the London Wall, you also have to tell Roman masonry from medieval additions; a single wall face in front of you often does not belong to one period.
