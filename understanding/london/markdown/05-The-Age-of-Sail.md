# Chapter Five — The Age of Sail: Merchantmen, privateers and the beginnings of empire

*1497 – 1600s*

> The Thames became the organising centre of English oceanic enterprise — and of its profits.

## How the Thames connected to the world

### 1. Voyages did not all start from London

John Cabot’s 1497 voyage to North America began at Bristol. Only in the later sixteenth century did London become the main organising centre of English oceanic enterprise, through merchant capital, royal charters, shipyards and the wharves of the Thames. The so-called “Age of Discovery” involved exploration, but also the search for trade routes, wartime reconnaissance and the contest for colonial footholds.

### 2. Royal chartered companies

A single voyage was costly and risky, so merchants pooled capital and shared losses. The Crown granted a company trade rights in a given region by charter: the Muscovy Company of 1555 traded with Russia; the East India Company of 1600 channelled London money into Asian trade. The Royal Exchange, opened in 1571, gave merchants a place to meet over exchange rates and long-distance news.

This system linked private capital to state power. A company could trade, but it could also build forts, conclude treaties and wage war, giving later English imperial expansion an organisational tool.

### 3. Drake’s circumnavigation

Francis Drake completed a circumnavigation in 1577–1580, and also engaged in privateering on Spanish colonial routes. He brought back spices and a great deal of wealth; England saw a hero of the sea, Spain a pirate. Navigation, national war and plunder are hard to separate here.

> **A small story · The queen boards at Deptford**
>
> In 1581 Elizabeth I boarded the *Golden Hind* on the Thames at Deptford, held a banquet and knighted Drake. The ceremony packaged a voyage full of risk and plunder as national honour, and showed that London’s port had become a stage on which monarchy, commerce and the navy performed together.

### 4. The other side of the wealth

English merchants took part in slave voyages early on, and London’s banking, insurance, shipping and processing industries later profited from colonial trade. Sugar, tobacco and cotton changed patterns of consumption, while the labour that produced them often came from enslaved people. Understanding how London became a world city means seeing navigational knowledge, commercial innovation, armed force and forced labour at the same time.
