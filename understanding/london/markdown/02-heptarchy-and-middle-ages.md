# Chapter Two — Heptarchy and Middle Ages: From Lundenwic to the Tower of London

*5th – 15th century*

> The city moved west, came back behind the walls, and met the Norman Conquest.

## The city moved west — and then back behind the walls

After the Roman period ended, Angles, Saxons, Jutes and others entered Britain in waves, passing through migration, war, intermarriage and assimilation with the local population. Only centuries later did what we now loosely call “Anglo-Saxon England” take shape.

### 1. The heptarchy and London

Later ages called the seven principal kingdoms the “Heptarchy”: Kent, Sussex, Wessex, Essex, East Anglia, Mercia and Northumbria. They did not stand neatly side by side for long, and smaller polities existed outside the seven throughout.

| Kingdom | Approximate location | Relationship to London |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Essex | East Saxon territory | Early London usually fell within its political sphere; St Paul’s became a bishop’s seat |
| Mercia | The English Midlands | Controlled London and its port repeatedly around the eighth century |
| Wessex | Southern and south-western England | Alfred re-founded London inside the walls around 886 |
| Kent | South of the Thames estuary | Faced London across the river; adopted Christianity comparatively early |
| Sussex | Southern England | Later came within Wessex’s sphere of influence |
| East Anglia | Eastern England | Shared North Sea trade and Viking networks with London |
| Northumbria | Northern England | Trade connected the northern kingdom with Lundenwic; an important centre of early English religious culture |

### 2. Lundenwic, the Vikings and Alfred

In the seventh to ninth centuries a new trading settlement, **Lundenwic**, grew up west of the Roman city, roughly around today’s Strand and Covent Garden. The element *wic* here means a trading settlement.

Viking raids changed the terms of security. Around 886 Alfred, king of Wessex, re-used the Roman walls and moved urban life back inside the better-defended circuit, creating **Lundenburh**. That London came successively under the influence of Essex, Mercia and Wessex shows how much more complicated the “Heptarchy” was than seven fixed territories.

> **A small story · Cnut orders the tide to turn back**
>
> Henry of Huntingdon, writing more than a century after Cnut’s death, tells how the king of England and Denmark set his throne on the shore and commanded the tide to stop. The rising water demonstrated the limits of earthly kingship before God. This is a later moral tale, not a securely documented event, and its location cannot be confidently identified as London.

### 3. The Norman Conquest and two centres of power

In 1066 William, Duke of Normandy, conquered England and was crowned at Westminster. The Tower of London, built soon after, defended against external enemies and displayed the new royal power to the city’s inhabitants. Westminster gradually became the seat of the royal court and of national politics, while the City of London grew on commerce, guilds and a tradition of self-government.

In the crisis over Magna Carta in 1215, London backed the barons against King John. Magna Carta was not a modern democratic constitution, but it confirmed London’s existing liberties — showing that a king had to take the city’s wealth and political weight seriously.
