# Chapter Three — The Tudor Reformation: Marriage, church and Puritans

*1509 – 1603; Stuart epilogue*

> A marital crisis helped drive the break with Rome, while the dissolution transformed the city’s religious landscape.

## How a royal marriage changed a nation and its capital

### 1. Henry VIII’s marital crisis

Henry VIII and his first wife, Catherine of Aragon, had only one child who survived to adulthood — a daughter, Mary. Fearing he would have no male heir, and now in love with Anne Boleyn, Henry asked the Pope to annul the marriage. The Pope refused; Catherine was also the aunt of Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor, which turned a royal marriage into a European political question.

In 1533 Henry married Anne, and Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury, declared the marriage to Catherine void. The **Act of Supremacy** of 1534 confirmed the king as the supreme head on earth of the Church of England, severing the English church’s institutional ties with Rome.

Henry married six times. His children later reigned in this order: Edward VI, son of Jane Seymour (1547–1553); Mary I, daughter of Catherine (1553–1558); and Elizabeth I, daughter of Anne (1558–1603). Succession, religious policy and personal marriage were thus tightly intertwined.

> **A small story · One fortress, two fates**
>
> In 1533 Anne Boleyn entered the Tower of London as queen consort ahead of her coronation; three years later she returned as a prisoner and was executed inside it. The Tower was both palace and prison, and the distance between the two could be a single political trial.

### 2. The Reformation was not just a divorce

After Henry broke with Rome, England did not immediately become a Protestant country in the later sense. He kept the episcopate and much traditional doctrine, but the church’s supreme judicial and financial authority passed to the Crown. Under Edward VI an English *Book of Common Prayer* was adopted and church images and altars were stripped away more radically; Mary I restored Catholicism and had Protestants who refused to conform executed at Smithfield and elsewhere; Elizabeth I rebuilt a Crown-led national church through the Act of Supremacy and the Act of Uniformity of 1559.

What these reversals changed was not only individual belief. Londoners had to learn new liturgical texts, new forms of oath and a new ordering of festivals; which church you obeyed also gradually became a political test of loyalty to the monarch.

### 3. After the monasteries vanished

Between 1536 and 1540 England’s monasteries were dissolved in stages, and their property, once it had passed to the Crown, was sold or granted to private owners, including nobles and urban elites. Monasteries had also provided medical care, alms and lodging, so the dissolution reshaped London’s networks of land and welfare. Place names such as Blackfriars, Whitefriars and Charterhouse still preserve religious houses that no longer exist.

The parish did not disappear. Churches handled baptism, marriage and burial, while parish meetings increasingly dealt with poor relief, roads, lighting and cleaning. London’s later administrative reforms would unfold precisely across this fine-grained network of parishes.

### 4. Mary, Elizabeth and the Tower

Under Edward VI Protestant reform continued; when Mary I came to the throne she restored the link with Rome. In 1554 Wyatt’s rebellion against Mary’s marriage to Philip, prince of Spain, failed. Mary suspected her half-sister Elizabeth of involvement and sent her to the Tower of London.

> **A small story · Mary I imprisons her sister Elizabeth in the Tower**
>
> Elizabeth entered the Tower of London by boat from the Thames. By later accounts she feared she would die there as her mother, Anne Boleyn, had done. The investigation found no evidence sufficient for conviction, and after two months she was moved to house arrest. Four years later Elizabeth inherited her sister’s throne; before her coronation she again stayed in the Tower by tradition — this time not as a prisoner but as queen.

### 5. The Puritans thought reform had not gone far enough — with a Stuart epilogue

Elizabeth I re-established a Protestant settlement under royal authority. A group of reformers thought the national church still retained too much Catholic ceremony and wanted simpler vestments, ornaments and worship, with more emphasis on preaching and the Bible. The label “Puritan” was already in use in the 1560s, often as an insult; it did not describe a single, doctrinally uniform sect.

In 1604 James I convened bishops of the national church and Puritan representatives at Hampton Court. He refused to abolish the episcopate but agreed to a new English translation of the Bible, which became the “King James Version”. By the reign of Charles I, ceremony, episcopal authority, taxation and the rights of Parliament had become entangled, and religious conflict became a major background to the civil war.
