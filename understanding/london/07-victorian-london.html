Chapter Seven

The Victorian Era

How an industrial metropolis worked

1837 – 1901

Beneath the grand capital: railways, sewers and a new kind of local government.

Beneath a grand capital: railways, sewers and local government

1. Victoria and Albert

Victoria came to the throne in 1837 and reigned until 1901. Buckingham Palace became the monarch’s principal London residence, but British politics was already run jointly by the monarch, the cabinet and Parliament according to law and convention.

The Great Exhibition of 1851 was closely associated with Prince Albert. Its surplus helped develop the South Kensington museums and educational quarter. After Albert’s death in 1861 Victoria withdrew from public appearances for a long period; the monarchy later rebuilt its public image step by step through commemoration, charity and state ceremony.

A small story · “Big Ben” is not the name of the tower

“Big Ben” originally referred to the largest striking bell inside the parliamentary clock tower. The tower itself was long known as the Clock Tower, and was formally renamed the Elizabeth Tower in 2012 to mark Elizabeth II’s Diamond Jubilee. Visitors calling the whole tower Big Ben has become common usage.

2. Cholera and the “Great Stink”

Explosive population growth turned drinking water and sewage into a crisis. In 1854 the physician John Snow plotted cholera cases in Soho on a map and found that they clustered around the Broad Street pump. In the hot summer of 1858 the stench of Thames sewage reached the point of disrupting business in Parliament, pushing the government to build a modern sewer system.

The engineer Joseph Bazalgette directed the construction of the main sewers and the embankments. Walking along the Thames Embankment today, you are standing on the engineering that transformed public health and urban traffic.

3. The Underground and new boundaries

In 1863 the Metropolitan Railway opened as the world’s first underground railway. Early trains used steam locomotives, and ventilation and smoke were problems passengers simply had to endure. The railways allowed residents to live further out and pushed London’s expansion into the suburbs.

At the start of the nineteenth century, metropolitan affairs were still handled separately by the City of London, Westminster and a host of parishes. The Metropolitan Board of Works was created in 1855, the London County Council began operating in 1889, and in 1900 twenty-eight metropolitan boroughs took shape. Parish boundaries went through several rounds of reform before gradually turning into a modern metropolitan administrative system.

4. A newly built “old London”

A fire in 1834 destroyed most of the old Palace of Westminster. The Houses of Parliament familiar today are largely nineteenth-century Gothic Revival buildings; only a few parts, such as Westminster Hall, are older. Tower Bridge, opened in 1894, likewise wears medieval clothing but is a modern piece of engineering built to serve a busy port.
